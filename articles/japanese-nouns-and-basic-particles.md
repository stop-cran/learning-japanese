# Japanese nouns and basic particles: building a sentence

Why does "at the library" use に in one sentence and で in another? Why can English "with" become either
と or で? Start with **the noun phrase's role in the whole sentence**, not a one-word translation of a particle.

Japanese nouns generally do not decline for **case**: they do not acquire different noun endings simply
because they are subjects, objects, or destinations. Instead, particles follow noun phrases and help show
their relationships. That does not mean Japanese has no changing endings: verbs, adjectives, and noun
predicates have their own patterns.

This article introduces the basic building blocks. The next lesson,
[は, が, and も](wa-ga-mo-topic-and-inclusion.md), develops topic, contrast, and inclusion in more detail.

## Noun phrase + particle, then a predicate

A **noun phrase** is a noun together with words belonging to it:

- [本](../words/本.md)（ほん）: a book.
- 私の本（わたしのほん）: my book.
- この本（このほん）: this book.

A particle follows the **whole phrase** it marks. The brackets below show the grouping; you do not write
them as part of the ordinary sentence:

> [私の本]を読みます。  
> わたしのほんを よみます。  
> I will read my book.

The book is what is read. を follows 私の本, not a separate English-style word meaning "my."
Inside that phrase, の connects 私 with 本; outside it, を gives the complete phrase its role with
[読む](../words/読む.md), "read."

A **predicate** says what happens, what something is, or what it is like. 読みます, "reads/will read,"
学生です, "is a student," and 大きいです, "is large," are all predicates.
In an ordinary simple Japanese clause, the predicate normally comes at the end.

> 妹が本を読みます。  
> いもうとが ほんを よみます。  
> My younger sister will read a book.

Here 妹が supplies the reader and 本を supplies the thing read. Do not identify those roles just by
counting which noun appears first. Noun phrases can sometimes be reordered, but that does not make every
order equally natural. An understood participant can also be left unspoken, as "I" is in the first example.

## A small map

These are selected uses, not complete definitions:

| Particle | Starting use in this lesson | Question to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| が | Subject of an ordinary action, existence, or state description | Who comes? What exists or has that property? |
| を | Object of a verb such as read, eat, or return | What is read, eaten, or returned? |
| に | Existence location, destination, time point, or a participant selected by the predicate | Which role does this particular predicate require? |
| で | Activity/event location or means | Where does it happen? Using what? |
| へ | Direction or destination of movement | Where is something heading? |
| と | Companion, or a link between named noun phrases | With whom? Or which nouns form a group? |
| の | A link from one noun expression to another | What kind of thing? Related to whom or what? |
| から | Starting point or source | From where or when? |
| まで | Endpoint or extent | As far as where? Until when? |

Three spellings need special attention: particle **は is pronounced wa**, **へ is pronounced e**, and
**を is pronounced o**. Keep the written particles は, へ, and を.
The other particles in the table have their ordinary kana readings.

Two more particles will appear in the examples: **は** sets up a topic or contrast, while **も** adds an
item to a relevant comparison. They do not simply name the same grammatical roles as が and を.

## When the predicate is a noun: だ and です

Suppose you are introducing yourself:

> 私は学生です。  
> わたしは がくせいです。  
> I am a student.

私は sets up you as the topic. [学生](../words/学生.md)（がくせい）, "student," supplies the description;
です makes this an ordinary polite noun-predicate statement.
The plain counterpart is 私は学生だ. The ending is often called a **copula**: it helps make a statement
identifying or classifying something, rather than naming an action.

Compare a few noun-predicate endings:

| Meaning | Plain form | Polite form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| am/is a student | 学生だ | 学生です |
| am/is not a student | 学生じゃない | 学生ではありません |
| was a student | 学生だった | 学生でした |

学生 stays the same; the material after it changes. じゃない is a common contraction of ではない,
and 学生じゃないです is also a common polite spoken negative. This is a small comparison, not every
negative, past, or conversational form.

Do not use だ or です as a universal equivalent of every English "be." For ordinary existence/location,
learn [いる](../words/いる.md) and [ある](../words/ある.md) below. Nor do you normally add です after
an ordinary positive verb form: 読みます is already polite; 読みますです is not the basic pattern.
The relationship between だ and です also differs with adjectives, which are outside this noun-ending table.

## が and を: who or what has which role?

In the reading example, が marks the subject and を marks the object. These roles are not interchangeable:

> 兄が妹を呼びます。  
> あにが いもうとを よびます。  
> My older brother will call my younger sister.
>
> 妹が兄を呼びます。  
> いもうとが あにを よびます。  
> My younger sister will call my older brother.

The same two people occur, but the particles identify who calls whom.
Here "call" means call to or summon someone, not specifically telephone them.

**A subject is not always a person doing an action:**

> 窓が大きいです。  
> まどが おおきいです。  
> The window is large.

Imagine noticing the window during a room tour. The window has the property; it is not an agent carrying
out an action. Similarly, in 猫がいます（ねこがいます）, "There is a cat," 猫 is what is present.
が is not a general word for "only"; identifying focus depends on the context.

**An object need not be physically changed.** 本を読みます marks the book as what is read, not something
that must be damaged or transformed.
Also learn the whole predicate pattern: 猫が好きです（ねこがすきです） means "I like cats."
The English object in "like cats" does not justify replacing が with を in the basic Japanese 好き pattern.
The [topic-particle lesson](wa-ga-mo-topic-and-inclusion.md) explains this boundary.

を has other uses too: 道を歩きます（みちをあるきます）, "I walk along the road," marks a route,
and 家を出ます（いえをでます）, "I leave the house," marks a place left behind.
Those are not ordinary objects being acted on. Learn them with their movement verbs rather than making
"を always means an affected object" an absolute rule.

## に and で: being somewhere versus doing something there

Return to the opening library question:

> 図書館にいます。  
> としょかんに います。  
> I am at the library.
>
> 図書館で勉強します。  
> としょかんで べんきょうします。  
> I study at the library.

The first locates someone; the second locates the activity of [studying](../words/勉強.md).
The English word "at" alone does not choose the Japanese particle.

### Locating a person, animal, or thing

For ordinary existence/location statements:

- **Place に + person/animal が + います**.
- **Place に + thing が + あります**.

> 庭に猫がいます。  
> にわに ねこが います。  
> There is a cat in the garden.
>
> 図書館に机があります。  
> としょかんに つくえが あります。  
> There is a desk at the library.

いる is the usual choice for people and animals; ある is the usual choice for things.
The examples use their polite forms, います and あります.
In each sentence, に marks the location and が marks what is there.

For ordinary **negation**, use いません／いない for a person or animal and ありません／ない for a thing.
先生は教室にいません（せんせいはきょうしつにいません） means "The teacher is not in the classroom";
部屋に机はありません（へやにつくえはありません） means "There is no desk in the room."
The topic or contrast particle は does not change the underlying choice of existence predicate.
Plants ordinarily take ある: 庭に花があります（にわにはながあります）, "There are flowers in the garden."
"Biologically alive versus not alive" is therefore an unreliable complete rule.
The [verb foundations](japanese-verb-foundations.md#make-the-negative-carefully) explain why the ordinary plain negative
of ある is ない, not あらない. The [adjective/noun bridge](japanese-adjective-foundations.md#a-noun-predicate-bridge)
adds noun-predicate past negatives such as 学生ではなかった; that is a different pattern from absence with いない.

### Locating an activity or event

> 公園で遊びます。  
> こうえんで あそびます。  
> I play in the park.

で locates the activity. Do not decide that every Japanese word translated as "in" must take に.

There is an important limit to a verb-only shortcut: ある can also mean that **an event takes place**.

> 学校で授業があります。  
> がっこうで じゅぎょうが あります。  
> There is a class at the school.

Here [授業](../words/授業.md) is a teaching session, not a classroom that exists somewhere.
で marks where that event takes place. Compare 学校に図書館があります
（がっこうに としょかんが あります）, "There is a library at the school."
Read the noun and the sense of the predicate together; do not turn "ある goes with に" into a universal rule.

### Tools, transport, and language: another で

| Phrase in a sentence | Meaning of the で phrase |
| --- | --- |
| ペンで書きます（ペンでかきます） | write **with a pen**: tool |
| 電車で行きます（でんしゃでいきます） | go **by train**: means of travel |
| 日本語で話します（にほんごではなします） | speak **in Japanese**: language used |

Compare **電車に乗ります**（でんしゃにのります）, "I board/ride a train."
[乗る](../words/乗る.md) selects に for the vehicle in this pattern; 行く can use 電車で to say how
you travel. The same train does not require one fixed particle in every sentence.

## Other basic に uses, and where へ overlaps

### Destination or direction

Both of these are ordinary ways to say that you are going to school:

> 学校に行きます。  
> がっこうに いきます。  
> I am going to school.
>
> 学校へ行きます。  
> がっこうへ いきます。  
> I am going to school.

The reading line preserves written へ; pronounce that particle **e**.
に presents the destination as a goal; へ presents movement in that direction or toward that destination.
With 行く, the difference is often small. Neither particle by itself guarantees that the trip is completed,
or asserts that the traveller will fail to arrive.

This overlap is **not** permission to replace every に with へ. 図書館にいます locates someone;
へ does not perform that existence-location job. Likewise, the time-point に below is not directional へ.

### A time point, not every time expression

> 三時に授業が始まります。  
> さんじに じゅぎょうが はじまります。  
> Class starts at three.

に marks the time at which the event starts. Expressions such as 今日（きょう）, "today," and
明日（あした）, "tomorrow," normally appear without に when they simply date an event:
明日行きます（あしたいきます）, "I will go tomorrow."
Do not add に after every English expression of time.

Also distinguish a clock time from a duration: 三時（さんじ） is three o'clock;
三時間（さんじかん） is three hours. 三時間勉強します（さんじかんべんきょうします） means
"I will study for three hours," not "I will study at three."

### A recipient or another predicate-specific participant

> 妹に本を貸します。  
> いもうとに ほんを かします。  
> I will lend a book to my younger sister.

With [貸す](../words/貸す.md), the person borrowing the book takes に; the book lent takes を.
The lender is understood here. This is not a claim that に always means "recipient": a different predicate
can assign a different role. Learn useful patterns such as **person に + thing を + 貸す** as a unit.

## と: a companion, or nouns joined into a group

First, accompaniment:

> 兄と図書館に行きます。  
> あにと としょかんに いきます。  
> I will go to the library with my older brother.

兄と marks the companion; 図書館に marks the destination. The traveller saying "I" is not separately
written, but is understood from the situation.

Now a noun list:

> 兄と妹が来ます。  
> あにと いもうとが きます。  
> My older brother and younger sister will come.

Group it as **[兄と妹]が**: と links the two nouns, and が marks the whole group as the subject.
Similarly, **[本とノート]を買います**（ほんとノートをかいます） means "I will buy books and notebooks."
This does not make と itself an "only" marker.

The same English "with" can describe a different role:

> 友達と手紙を書きます。  
> ともだちと てがみを かきます。  
> I will write a letter with a friend.
>
> ペンで手紙を書きます。  
> ペンで てがみを かきます。  
> I will write a letter with a pen.

The friend participates; the pen is a tool. This answers the opening "with" question without assigning
one Japanese particle to every English preposition.

For named versus representative lists, や, とか, など, and listing activities, continue with
[Japanese listing and examples](japanese-listing-and-examples.md).
Quotation と and conditional と are different uses, not extra meanings to force into every companion or list.

## の: relationships inside a noun phrase

The basic pattern is **N1 の N2**. N2 is the main noun; N1 helps specify it.
Possession is one possible relationship, not the whole meaning:

| Noun phrase | Reading | A natural interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 私の本 | わたしのほん | my book: possession |
| 学校の先生 | がっこうのせんせい | a teacher at the school: affiliation |
| 木の机 | きのつくえ | a wooden desk: material |
| 三時の電車 | さんじのでんしゃ | the three-o'clock train: time |

The words and context supply the particular relationship. の does not secretly mean "owns" in every row.

Spatial nouns use the same inner linking pattern: **机の上**（つくえのうえ）, "on top of the desk,"
**銀行の後ろ**（ぎんこうのうしろ）, "behind the bank," and **学校の外**（がっこうのそと）,
"outside the school." Add a particle for the whole phrase's role:
机の上に本があります, "There is a book on the desk," but 学校の外で待ちます,
"I wait outside the school." の builds the phrase; に or で relates it to the predicate.
Here 待ちます is まちます, "wait."

The whole expression can then take another particle:

> 学校の先生が来ます。  
> がっこうの せんせいが きます。  
> A teacher from the school will come.

The subject is **[学校の先生]**, not 学校 by itself. This is the same inner-phrase/outer-role distinction
as **[私の本]を** at the beginning.
Not every Japanese modifier needs の: この本 already means "this book"; do not make このの本.

**Nominalizing の is a separate pattern.** It can make a verb phrase or clause noun-like rather than simply
linking two nouns. For that use and its relationship with こと, see
[koto, wake, and Japanese noun-based grammar](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md).

## から and まで: a starting point and an endpoint

For a route:

> 家から駅まで歩きます。  
> いえから えきまで あるきます。  
> I will walk from home to the station.

から marks the starting place; まで marks how far this walk extends.
They do not have to appear as a pair: 駅まで歩きます, "I will walk to the station," can leave the
starting point to the context.

For a time interval:

> 九時から五時まで働きます。  
> くじから ごじまで はたらきます。  
> I work from nine to five.

These are the start and end of the stated working period. The sentence does not specify every break
or detail within that schedule.
から can also introduce a reason after a clause, but that is not the noun-following source use being taught here.

### まで is not the same task as までに

Compare an activity continuing up to a time with an action due by that time:

> 三時まで勉強します。  
> さんじまで べんきょうします。  
> I will study until three.
>
> 三時までに本を返してください。  
> さんじまでに ほんを かえしてください。  
> Please return the book by three.

In the first sentence, three is the endpoint of the study period. In the second, three is the deadline
for [returning the book](../words/返す.md); an earlier return satisfies the request too.
The return is not an activity that must continue until three.

This is why swapping まで and までに can change the task, not merely its style.
The distinction here concerns these time-limit patterns; まで also has other uses, including emphatic "even."
For a real deadline, specify the actual time rather than relying on an ambiguous expression such as "by summer."

## Where は and も fit

A grammatical role and the way you present information are different layers:

> この本を読みました。  
> このほんを よみました。  
> I read this book.
>
> この本は読みました。  
> このほんは よみました。  
> I read this book, as for this one.
>
> この本も読みました。  
> このほんも よみました。  
> I read this book too.

The middle translation deliberately exposes the topic/contrast framing; ordinary English may just say
"I read this book." In all three, the book is what was read, not the reader.
In these basic object patterns, は or も replaces を rather than turning the book into the subject.

With other case relations, the ordinary full form can keep the case particle:
図書館では（としょかんでは）, "at the library, as a topic/contrast," and
図書館でも（としょかんでも）, "at the library too."
Do not mechanically delete every case particle or add は/も after every one.
These case combinations differ from the ending ではありません in the noun-predicate table.

The [は／が／も lesson](wa-ga-mo-topic-and-inclusion.md) develops these choices, including identifying
answers, negative inclusion, and why a contrast need not state the opposite about an unmentioned item.

## A practical way to choose a particle

1. Find the predicate and its intended sense: being somewhere, studying, lending, riding, or something else.
2. Identify the complete noun phrase, including any の relationship or noun list inside it.
3. Identify its role in that pattern: subject, object, location, destination, companion, means, or time.
4. Check whether topic, contrast, or inclusion changes how that phrase is presented.
5. Read the completed sentence. A different particle may change the participant's role, not just add a small nuance.

Do not assume an isolated sentence always has exactly one possible particle. 学校に行きます and
学校へ行きます are both natural, while 電車に乗ります and 電車で行きます illustrate different
predicate patterns. Context, the intended role, and the complete construction matter together.

This introduction does not cover every case-particle sense, passive/causative patterns, relative-clause が/の,
all conversational particle omissions, or all noun-predicate endings. Those are later topics, not exceptions
that must be memorized before using these basic sentences.
