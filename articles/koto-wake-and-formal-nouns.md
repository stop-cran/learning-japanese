# Koto, wake, and Japanese noun-based grammar

**こと presents an action or situation as something you can talk about. わけ relates a situation to a reason, explanation, or conclusion.** They belong to a broader family of noun-based grammatical expressions, but they are not interchangeable.

## The terminology: formal nouns and nominalizers

The usual Japanese term is **形式名詞（けいしきめいし, keishiki meishi）**, commonly translated as **formal nouns**. The term **dependent nouns** also appears in descriptions of Japanese, though the categories do not always have identical boundaries.

Here, "formal" means that a noun serves a grammatical function; it does not mean that the word belongs to polite or formal speech.

These nouns have relatively abstract or weakened lexical meaning in the relevant uses. A preceding expression supplies their content:

| Expression | A useful literal interpretation | Natural English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 日本語を勉強すること | the activity of studying Japanese | studying Japanese |
| 窓を開けたまま | in the unchanged state resulting from opening the window | with the window left open |
| 私が知っているかぎり | within the limits of what I know | as far as I know |

They retain noun-like grammar. Depending on the construction, the resulting phrases can take particles such as は, が, を, and に, or be followed by だ. The whole expression may function like an English noun phrase, an adverbial clause, or an expression of expectation or explanation.

**Nominalizer** names a function, not the entire family: it is something that turns a verb phrase or clause into a noun-like expression. This is a central use of こと and の, but it is not an adequate description of every expression containing わけ or まま.

The boundaries of 形式名詞 vary across grammatical descriptions. In particular, time words such as とき and ころ retain substantial ordinary noun meaning. **Noun-based grammatical expressions** is a useful broad description for the whole group, rather than a single universally agreed part-of-speech label.

A noun does not become a formal noun merely because a clause precedes it:

> 私が買った本  
> The book I bought.

Here, 本 is an ordinary noun modified by 私が買った.

## こと: the action, event, fact, or content

As a nominalizer, こと makes an action or clause usable where a noun phrase is needed:

> 私の趣味は本を読むことです。  
> My hobby is reading books.

> 彼が来ないことは知っている。  
> I know that he is not coming.

In the second example, the object of knowledge is the **fact itself**, not necessarily the reason behind it.

Thinking of こと as "the fact that" can help with factual clauses, but that translation does not fit every use. In 本を読むこと, "reading books" or "the activity of reading books" is more appropriate. Nominalization does not by itself assert that an event actually happened.

## わけ: a reason, or a situation understood through reasoning

### The ordinary noun meaning

As an ordinary noun, **[わけ](../words/訳.md)** can mean "reason" or "circumstances".
It can also be written **[訳](../kanji/訳.md)**; kana is common in grammatical constructions:

> 彼が来ないわけは知っている。  
> I know why he is not coming.

This gives a useful direct contrast:

| Sentence | What is known? |
| --- | --- |
| 彼が来ない**こと**は知っている。 | That he is not coming. |
| 彼が来ない**わけ**は知っている。 | Why he is not coming. |

The second sentence uses the relatively concrete "reason" meaning of わけ. It is an entry point to understanding the word, not a complete explanation of the grammatical construction わけだ.

### The explanatory construction わけだ

In **clause + わけだ**, the speaker presents the clause as an understandable consequence, a conclusion, or a restatement of the situation:

> A: 彼は日本に十年住んでいました。  
> He lived in Japan for ten years.
>
> B: なるほど、日本語が上手なわけだ。  
> Ah, no wonder his Japanese is good.

**日本語が上手なわけだ does not mean "the reason his Japanese is good."** It means that his Japanese being good makes sense in light of the information just provided.

Another common use is to spell out what some information amounts to:

> 一日二時間、五日間勉強した。つまり、合計十時間勉強したわけだ。  
> I studied for two hours a day for five days. In other words, I studied for ten hours in total.

Useful English equivalents include "no wonder," "so that means," and "in other words." None is a universal translation. わけだ can also be used to develop an explanation without expressing a dramatic new realization.

## How the preceding expression attaches

Use the appropriate **noun-modifying form**, not an unchanged polite sentence: 読みます becomes **読むこと**, and 行きました becomes **行ったこと**. The connector depends on both the preceding expression and the following word.

These are common **affirmative nonpast** patterns, not a universal attachment rule for every member of the family:

| Preceding expression | Pattern | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verb | Plain form directly before the noun | 読むこと, 読むわけ, 読むとき |
| I-adjective | Plain form directly before the noun | 高いこと, 高いわけ, 高いとき |
| Na-adjective | な before these nouns | 静かなこと, 上手なわけ, 静かなとき |
| Noun with とき, ころ, まま, or ため | の | 学生のとき, 子供のころ, 昔のまま, 雨のため |
| Noun predicate with explanatory わけ | な, or a formulation with という | 学生なわけだ, 学生というわけだ |
| Noun predicate nominalized with こと | である | 学生であること |

The last row avoids a useful trap: **学生であること** means "being a student," whereas **学生のこと** concerns a student or students: "matters about the student(s)." They do not mean the same thing.

Past and negative predicates keep their appropriate plain-form endings: **行ったこと**, **学生だったわけ**, and **静かではないとき**. Do not insert an extra な or の after those endings.

## Learn the whole construction

The words' central meanings help, but particles and surrounding verbs determine the construction:

| Construction | Example | Meaning in this example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dictionary/ない-form verb + ことにする | 毎日歩くことにした。 | I decided to walk every day. |
| Dictionary/ない-form verb + ことになる | 来月から大阪で働くことになった。 | It has been decided that I will work in Osaka from next month. |
| た-form verb (plain past) + ことがある | 京都に行ったことがある。 | I have been to Kyoto. |
| Dictionary-form verb + ことができる | 日本語を読むことができる。 | I can read Japanese. |
| Dictionary-form verb + ことはない | 心配することはない。 | There is no need to worry. |
| Clause + わけだ | 日本語が上手なわけだ。 | No wonder their Japanese is good. |
| Clause + わけがない | 彼がそんなことを言うわけがない。 | There is no way he would say that. |
| Clause + わけではない | 日本語が嫌いなわけではない。 | It is not that I dislike Japanese. |
| Dictionary/ない-form verb + わけにはいかない | 約束したので、休むわけにはいかない。 | I made a promise, so I cannot take time off. |

**ことにする versus ことになる** is a difference of framing, not an absolute rule about who made the decision. ことにする directly
presents an act of choosing; ことになる presents a resulting situation or arrangement. The latter often reports a decision made
elsewhere, but can also announce a personal decision indirectly. It can express a consequence without anyone making a decision:
**一袋に六個なら、三袋で十八個あることになる**, "If there are six in one bag, three bags amount to eighteen."

**ことができる** covers ability and possibility provided by circumstances or rules, not only physical skill.
With **ことがある**, the preceding verb matters: **行ったことがある** presents past experience, whereas **行くことがある**
describes occasions when one goes. These are not interchangeable tense forms.

The last わけ example concerns obligations or circumstances that make an action unacceptable, rather than a lack of physical ability. Also, ことはない has other uses: the "no need to" interpretation above should not be applied mechanically to every occurrence.

The three negative わけ constructions are especially important to keep separate:

- **わけがない** strongly rejects a possibility.
- **わけではない** rejects a characterization or inference; with an appropriate context, it can express "not necessarily" or "not always."
- **わけにはいかない** says that circumstances, duties, or social considerations prevent an action.

Negating the embedded verb changes the last pattern: **行かないわけにはいかない** means "I cannot very well not go," or, in context, "I have to go."

There is overlap between larger expressions. **～ということだ** can also mean "that means ...," for example. "こと concerns content; わけ concerns explanatory reasoning" is a useful starting point, not a rule that こと can never appear in a conclusion.

## Common formal nouns and close grammatical neighbors

This is a broad practical inventory for modern Japanese, not a universally exhaustive list. **Classification belongs to a use, not automatically to every occurrence of a word.** The groups below organize meanings, not separate parts of speech; time and relational nouns often retain more ordinary noun meaning than こと or はず.

Each row gives a starting point and one translated example, not every construction or an unrestricted substitution rule. The particle neighbors have their own section below. Classical-only uses and a complete inventory of particles are outside this article's scope.

### Content, expectations, and states

| Word | Central idea in these uses | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **[こと](../words/こと.md)** (koto) | An action, event, fact, or content | 本を読むことが好きだ。 - I like reading books. |
| **もの** (mono) | The general nature of things or characteristic behavior | 人は変わるものだ。 - People change; that is how people are. |
| **わけ** (wake) | An explanation or conclusion | なるほど、忙しいわけだ。 - Ah, no wonder you are busy. |
| **ところ** (tokoro) | A stage or point in an action | 今、出るところだ。 - I am just about to leave. |
| **[はず](../words/はず.md)** (hazu) | Expectation based on reasons | 彼は来るはずだ。 - I expect him to come. |
| **[つもり](../words/つもり.md)** (tsumori) | Intention; in other uses, one's own understanding | 行くつもりだ。 - I intend to go. |
| **[まま](../words/まま.md)** (mama) | A state remaining unchanged | 窓を開けたまま寝た。 - I slept with the window left open. |
| **とおり** (toori) | Correspondence with a model or instruction | 言われたとおりにした。 - I did as instructed. |
| **ほう** (hou) | One alternative or side of a comparison | 電車で行くほうが安い。 - Going by train is cheaper. |

### ものだ: characteristic patterns and remembered habits

**ものだ／ものです** can present a pattern as familiar, characteristic or naturally expected.
That is different from simply naming a physical object with もの:

> 人は、長く使わない言葉を少しずつ忘れるものです。  
> People tend to gradually forget words they have not used for a long time.

Here the speaker characterizes a tendency, not an obligation to forget or a rule guaranteed for every person and word.
Other ものだ sentences can be normative; the complete context matters.

With a **た-form verb**, ものだ can look back on a recurring past experience, often with nostalgia or reflection:

> 学生のころは、よく傘を電車に忘れたものです。  
> I remember often leaving my umbrella on the train when I was a student.
>
> 昔は夏祭りの夜になると、みんなで広場に集まったものです。  
> We used to gather in the square on summer festival nights.

The first memory need not be a pleasant one. Nor does this construction alone prove that the old pattern has completely
ceased. It is more than merely reporting that something has happened at least once with **たことがある**.
Past form alone does not settle every ものだ reading; the repeated occasions and reflective context are important here.

The pattern/memory questions supply **ものです** and explicitly request only the preceding verb's dictionary or た form.
For example, **降るものです** characterizes snowfall, while **降ったものです** can recall past winters.
This is controlled practice of those readings, not a full account of every use of ものだ.

### ほう and ほか: compare alternatives or add something else

**AよりBのほうが...** describes B in comparison with A. The predicate determines the property;
ほう does not itself mean "bigger" or "better":

| Example | Relationship |
| --- | --- |
| 赤いかばんより青いかばんのほうが軽い。 | The blue bag is lighter than the red one. |
| 昼より夜のほうが涼しい。 | Nighttime is cooler than daytime. |
| 歩くよりバスに乗るほうが早い。 | Taking the bus gets you there sooner than walking. |

After a noun, use **のほう**; after a verb clause, attach **ほう** directly.
**方** is read **ほう** in this comparison, not **かた** as in **読み方**, "how to read."
The comparison standard can also be understood from context rather than always stated with より.

**[ほか](../words/ほか.md)** can add something outside the item already named:

> パンのほかに、ケーキも売っています。  
> Besides bread, the shop also sells cakes.
>
> 日本語を教えているほかに、通訳の仕事もしています。  
> In addition to teaching Japanese, I also work as an interpreter.

Use **noun + のほかに** or **verb clause + ほかに**. Here ほか can be written **他／外**.
Addition does not mean one item replaces the other or ranks above it.
The rest of the sentence matters: **私のほかに誰もいません**, "there is nobody here except me,"
excludes other people rather than asserting that another person is present.
Other expressions, including うえに in suitable sentences, can add information too.

### ほうがいい: recommend doing or not doing

| Advice | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| た-form + ほうがいい | 先に予約したほうがいい。 | You should reserve in advance. |
| ない-form + ほうがいい | まだ予約しないほうがいい。 | You should not reserve yet. |

The first construction need not refer to the past: **明日行ったほうがいい**, "you should go tomorrow,"
is advice about a future trip. The た-form includes endings in **だ**, as in **読んだほうがいい**.
For the basic negative advice pattern, use **行かない／読まない**, not simply a past negative form.

Dictionary-form **行くほうがいい** can also compare choices or express what would be better.
The quiz's advice questions explicitly request **た** or **ない** forms; the narrower answer list is not a claim that
every dictionary-form example is wrong.
ほうがいい can sound direct, and it is not automatically a gentle request or a rule binding everyone.
Advice with **べき**, **ことだ** and other expressions has overlapping territory, with context-dependent differences;
learn the whole sentence rather than equating every English "should" with one construction.

### Intention, expectation, and what ought to happen

English "supposed to" can hide different meanings. **つもり** can state what someone intends an action or object to mean;
**はず** expresses an expectation supported by reasons. **べき** is a useful auxiliary-grammar comparison here, not another
formal noun: it expresses what ought to be done rather than predicting what will happen.

| Construction | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun + のつもりで | これは犬のつもりで作った。 | I made this intending it to be a dog. |
| Noun + のはずだ | ラベルからすると、中身は水のはずだ。 | Judging by the label, the contents should be water. |
| Dictionary-form verb + べきだ | 約束の時間を守るべきだ。 | One ought to keep to the agreed time. |

The first use of つもり concerns the maker's intention, not whether the result is convincing. It is related to, but not identical
with, the future-plan use **行くつもりだ**. With はず, match the connector to the preceding expression: **水のはず**,
**静かなはず**, **高いはず**, **来るはず**. An expectation is not a wish or a guarantee of what you will actually observe.

**ようだ** can also express an information-based judgment, including from indirect information: **水のようだ** can mean
"It seems to be water," not just "It resembles water." Whether you have directly inspected a property does not by itself settle
the choice between はず and よう. Their uses can overlap without having identical nuance or a fixed difference in confidence.

**べき** can sound forceful; it is not the default for every friendly suggestion. **行くべきではない** says one ought not to go,
whereas **行くべきだった** says one ought to have gone. In the latter, the verb remains 行く and the past appears on だった.
For する, both **するべき** and **すべき** are possible.

Some normative statements also allow **ものだ／ものではない**, and **ことだ** can give advice or instructions. These are not
unrestricted replacements for べき: judge the complete sentence and its communicative purpose. Likewise, **ものではない** can
reject a generalization rather than prohibit conduct, as in a discussion of what does not necessarily happen.

**のだ／んだ** can also urge an action, and **のではない／んではない** can prohibit one. With an appropriate retrospective
context, **調べておくのだった／調べておくんだった** can mean "I should have checked beforehand," expressing regret without
necessarily asserting a past plan. These forms have other uses too, including explanation, denial and recalling a forgotten plan;
they are not automatic substitutes for べき in every sentence.

### つもり: a plan or a belief about what has been done

The verb before **つもり** and the ending after it do different jobs:

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dictionary form + つもりです | 来年、自転車を買うつもりです。 | I plan to buy a bicycle next year. |
| ない form + つもりです | 今年は新しい自転車を買わないつもりです。 | I intend not to buy a new bicycle this year. |
| Dictionary form + つもりでした | 昨日は申込書を送るつもりでした。 | I intended to send the application yesterday. |
| た form + つもりでした | 申込書を送ったつもりでした。 | I thought I had sent the application. |

Use a **plain verb form** before つもり, even with polite **です／でした** afterward.
A plan need not be imminent: the bicycle purchase can be next year. Intending an action does not guarantee carrying it out.
**買わないつもり** states a plan not to buy; it does not itself mean inability to buy.
**買うつもりはない／買うつもりがない** instead denies having the intention to buy.
The formulations can overlap in practical use; do not choose between them by a universal strength-of-refusal scale.

Keep the two past expressions separate. In **送るつもりでした**, **でした** places the intention in the past;
the intended sending is not changed into **送った** merely because we are now looking back.
In **送ったつもりでした**, the sending is presented as completed **within the speaker's belief**.
The actual outcome may differ: pressing a send button does not prove that an application was successfully transmitted.
With a present belief, the ending can instead be **送ったつもりです**, "I believe I have sent it."

The rest of the account tells us what actually happened. A past intention can be carried out:
**月曜日には、金曜日に予約するつもりでした。実際に金曜日に予約しました。**
"On Monday, I intended to make the reservation on Friday. I actually did so on Friday."
Likewise, a belief in completion need not be false:
**窓を全部閉めたつもりでした。あとで確認すると、やはり全部閉まっていました。**
"I believed I had closed all the windows. When I checked later, they were indeed all closed."
つもり foregrounds the speaker's mental stance, rather than independently verifying the result.

The plan questions supply **つもりです** and request only the given verb's dictionary or ない form.
The past-viewpoint questions all supply **つもりでした** and request dictionary or た form, with the intended mental
stance stated before answering. They practice those controlled contrasts, not unrestricted rejection of other ways to
express intention or belief. The next subsection treats self-assessment with noun and adjective predicates.

### つもり: how I see myself versus what I intend to become

Not every **つもりです** means a future plan. With a noun or adjective, it can describe how someone sees themselves:

| Basic attachment | Current self-assessment | Intended change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun + のつもり | 自分ではプロのつもりです。 - I regard myself as a professional. | 来年はプロになるつもりです。 - I intend to turn professional next year. |
| な-adjective + なつもり | 自分では料理が上手なつもりです。 - I consider myself good at cooking. | 練習して、もっと上手になるつもりです。 - I intend to practice and become more skilled. |
| い-adjective + つもり | 自分では将棋が強いつもりです。 - I consider myself a strong shogi player. | 次の大会までにはもっと強くなるつもりです。 - I intend to become stronger by the next tournament. |

**将棋（しょうぎ）** is Japanese chess; **上手（じょうず）** means skillful.
For the basic self-assessment forms, link a noun with **の**, a な-adjective with **な**, and an い-adjective directly.
Do not add another の or な after **強い**.
For a change, use **noun／な-adjective + になる** or **い-adjective's く form + なる**:
**専門家になる**, "become an expert"; **慎重になる**, "become careful"; **詳しくなる**, "become knowledgeable."
The resulting plain verb phrase then attaches to つもり.

The same outer **つもりです** can report a current self-view or a current intention whose goal lies in the future.
It is the predicate and context, not です alone, that tell you which.
**プロのつもり** foregrounds the speaker's professional self-image; **プロになるつもり** explicitly makes becoming
a professional the intended change. These are not a universal rule that every noun/adjective つもり concerns only the present:
other contexts can describe an intended role or an assumed situation.

A self-assessment is a perspective, not independent proof. Someone may regard their cooking as good while their family
finds the seasoning too strong. Other information may instead support the assessment, such as clients treating a photographer
as a professional or a player winning tournaments. Do not infer from つもり alone that the speaker must be mistaken.
Likewise, a plan to become more skilled does not guarantee the eventual result.
Knowingly acting as if something were true is another use: **親のつもりで世話をする**, "look after someone as if one
were their parent," describes an adopted role rather than a professional qualification or a future plan.

The exercises use six matched predicates: two nouns (**専門家／プロ**), two な-adjectives (**上手／慎重**) and
two い-adjectives (**詳しい／強い**). **専門家（せんもんか）** means expert, **慎重（しんちょう）** careful,
and **詳しい（くわしい）** knowledgeable in these contexts.
Each question names the two attachment patterns, supplies **つもりです**, and requests the missing predicate span.
These are controlled form-and-meaning tasks, not an unrestricted choice among every way to express self-belief or intention.
Other formulations such as **自分はプロだと思っています**, "I think of myself as a professional," remain possible.
For the change verb, kana **なる** is usual; dictionary spellings **成る／為る** are accepted without recommending them
as equally common or clear. The uncommon spellings **委しい／精しい** of **詳しい** are also accepted, including their
**く** forms with the supported spellings of なる; the models keep ordinary **詳しい／詳しくなる**.
The next subsection practices denying an intention with **つもりはない／つもりがない** and related forms.

### つもり: negative plans, denied intentions and actual outcomes

Negation can appear before **つもり** or after it. The structures foreground different things:

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vない + つもりだ | このギターは売らないつもりだ。 | I intend not to sell this guitar. |
| Vる + つもりは／がない | このギターを売るつもりはない。 | I have no intention of selling this guitar. |
| Vる + つもりでは／じゃない | このギターを売るつもりじゃない。 | Selling this guitar is not my intention. |

**売る（うる）** means sell; its ない form is **売らない**.
The first pattern puts not selling inside the plan. The second denies having the selling intention;
the third can correct or qualify what someone takes the intention to be.
**じゃ** is the conversational contraction of **では**. **つもりがない** is also possible with a first-person speaker;
it is not reserved for reports about other people.

If someone assumes you will sell the guitar, but you have decided to keep playing it yourself, all three patterns can fit.
The same practical choice can be described as a negative plan or as a denied intention.
This does not make every absence of a plan a deliberate decision to avoid something.
Nor are the formulations interchangeable in every context: **つもりはない** can sound like a firm refusal,
while **つもりじゃない** often qualifies an apparent intention. Do not grade an otherwise fitting answer by a universal
stronger/weaker ranking.

The first six exercises name the basic plain patterns and ask for the whole verb-plus-つもり expression.
Three shared negative answers contrast with three affirmative intentions, using **売る**, **借りる（かりる, borrow）**
and **買う（かう, buy）** on both sides. The chosen negative plans and corrected assumptions make the alternatives coherent.
The model button is just one representative; the other accepted formulations are not wrong distractors.
These are not unrestricted paraphrase questions.

In the past, keep the action and the intention separate:

| Intention at that time | Example | Actual outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Denied | そのファイルを消すつもりはなかった。でも、操作を間違えて消してしまった。 - I did not intend to delete that file, but made an operating mistake and deleted it. | The unintended action happened. |
| Affirmed | そのファイルを消すつもりだった。でも、削除する権限がなかった。 - I intended to delete that file, but did not have permission to delete it. | The intended action could not be carried out. |

**消す（けす）／削除する（さくじょする）** mean delete here; **権限（けんげん）** means authority or permission.
**つもりはなかった／がなかった** denies the past intention; **つもりではなかった／じゃなかった** rejects the action
as the intention. These alternatives fit the accidental-action examples without denying what actually happened.
**つもりだった** affirms a past intention, whether or not it succeeded. One exercise explicitly recalls a morning plan
to move a vase and confirms that it was carried out that afternoon.

The six past questions print **dictionary-form verb + つもり** and request only **だった** or
**は／が／では／じゃなかった**. The English instruction names the intended mental stance before answering.
Do not change the printed verb to its た form: **消すつもりだった** reports an earlier intention, whereas
**消したつもりだった** reports a past belief that deletion had been completed.
A whole-sentence **捨てないつもりだった**, "I intended not to throw it away," may also be natural in a keeping-plan context,
but it cannot fill the blank in **捨てるつもり____**. Denying an intention does not by itself establish a prior avoidance plan.

### ところ: locating an action's stage

For activities such as writing a letter, the verb form before **ところ** distinguishes three stages:

| Form | Example | Stage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dictionary-form verb + ところだ／です | 手紙を書くところです。 | I am just about to write a letter. |
| て-form verb + いるところだ／です | 手紙を書いているところです。 | I am in the middle of writing a letter. |
| た-form verb + ところだ／です | 手紙を書いたところです。 | I have just written a letter. |

Use the plain verb form before ところ even when the sentence ends in polite **です**. For example, 書く becomes **書いている**
in the ongoing pattern and **書いた** in the completed pattern; 読む becomes **読んでいる／読んだ**.
In conversation, the い in ている／でいる may be omitted: **書いてるところです**, "I am in the middle of writing,"
or **読んでるところです**, "I am in the middle of reading." The uncontracted forms are the neutral learning models.

Context identifies the relevant action and its stage. Opening a book can be finished while reading it has not yet begun.
Likewise, finishing one bite does not mean the whole meal is over. **これから**, "from now," and **たった今**, "just now,"
often help locate the stage, but read the whole situation rather than treating these words as automatic answer keys.

**食べたところです**, "I have just eaten," overlaps with **食べたばかりです**, "I have only just eaten."
ところ focuses on the point just after the action; ばかり presents the event as still recent from the speaker's perspective,
even when more time has passed. Both can fit an immediately completed meal: this is not a contrast where one must always be wrong.

This table covers the three basic activity patterns, not every use of ところ. Nor does **ている** always mean an action is underway:
it can also describe a resulting state. A past reference point can appear on the final copula, as in
**電話が鳴ったとき、手紙を書いているところでした**, "When the phone rang, I was in the middle of writing a letter."

### Time, recurrence, and occasions

| Word | Central idea in these uses | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **うち** (uchi) | Within a period, or while an opportunity lasts | 忘れないうちに - before I forget |
| **あいだ** (aida) | An interval during which something happens | 待っているあいだ - while waiting |
| **とき** (toki) | A time or occasion | 困ったとき - when in trouble |
| **[ころ](../words/ころ.md)** (koro) | An approximate time or a period of life | 子供のころ - when I was a child |
| **まえ** (mae) | Before an event | 寝るまえに歯を磨く。 - I brush my teeth before going to bed. |
| **あと** (ato) | After an event | 食べたあとで散歩した。 - I took a walk after eating. |
| **たび** (tabi) | Each occurrence of an event | 会うたびに新しい話を聞く。 - Every time we meet, I hear a new story. |
| **おり** (ori) | An occasion or opportunity | 東京に行ったおりに、友人を訪ねた。 - On a visit to Tokyo, I visited a friend. |
| **さい** (sai) | An occasion or situation, often in formal notices | 入国のさいに、パスポートを見せた。 - I showed my passport when entering the country. |

For the basic event-order patterns, use **dictionary-form verb + まえに**, but **た-form verb + あとで／あとに**.
These forms locate one event relative to another, not necessarily relative to the present:

| Time of the whole situation | Example | Why this verb form? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Past report | 昨日は食べるまえに机をふきました。 - Yesterday I wiped the table before eating. | 食べる remains dictionary form before まえ, despite the past main clause. |
| Future plan | 明日は食べたあとで出かけます。 - Tomorrow I will go out after eating. | 食べた marks eating as completed relative to going out, even though both events are future. |

With event nouns, use **の**: **授業のまえに／授業のあとに**, "before/after class."
あと does not itself mean "immediately afterward"; it can describe an action later in the sequence with a gap between events.
With suitable verbs and context, **とき** can also locate the main action before or after the other action's completion.
Do not assume that every とき sentence describes two strictly simultaneous actions.

### うち and あいだ: a window, a developing change, or a continuing state

**うちに** can present a state as an opportunity that may end:

> 店が混まないうちに、買い物を済ませよう。  
> Let's finish shopping before the shop gets crowded.

Here **ないうちに** means before the change occurs, while the negative state still holds. It does not assert that the
change will definitely happen. An affirmative state also works: **店がすいているうちに**, "while the shop is not crowded."
Common attachment patterns include **明るいうちに**, "while it is light," **元気なうちに**, "while one is healthy,"
and **学生のうちに**, "while one is a student."

With an ongoing or repeated activity, **うちに** can instead locate a change that develops during it:

> 新しい町を毎日歩いているうちに、少しずつ道を覚えた。  
> Through walking around the new town each day, I gradually learned my way.

This is not a command to finish something before an opportunity disappears. Both dictionary-form and **ている** verbs
can occur before うちに; choose according to the whole expression. The negative pattern also has uses beyond warnings
about a missed opportunity.

**あいだに** locates an event within an interval. **あいだは** makes the interval a topic, often contrasting it with afterward:

| Example | Framing |
| --- | --- |
| 会議のあいだに、九時半に電話が来た。 | A call came at 9:30 within the meeting interval. |
| 会議のあいだは、隣の部屋でずっと待っていた。 | During the meeting, I kept waiting in the next room: that interval is the topic. |

The familiar **あいだ** versus **あいだに** contrast is a useful starting point for a continuing action versus an event
within its duration. It is not a mechanical test that makes every alternative particle ungrammatical: **は** can introduce
an interval even when the following sentence reports a single event. Learn what the speaker is choosing to foreground.
Use **の** after an event noun, as in 会議のあいだ; a verb phrase attaches directly, as in **寝ているあいだ**, "while sleeping."

**うちに**, **あいだに** and **ときに** can overlap. うちに often brings out the possibility that a useful state will end;
あいだに locates something within a period. This difference in emphasis does not make them mutually exclusive answers
in every sentence about acting while there is time.

### たび, おり and さい: recurrence versus an occasion

**たびに** explicitly associates something with each occurrence of an action or event:

> あの友達に会うたびに、新しい話を聞く。  
> Every time I meet that friend, I hear a new story.

For the basic recurrence pattern, use **dictionary-form verb + たびに**, or **event noun + のたびに**,
as in **出張のたびに**, "on every business trip."
The repeated result can be pleasant or unwelcome; たび does not itself mean gradual change.

**おりに** and **さいに** refer to occasions and often suit more formal wording than **ときに**:

> 京都を訪ねたおりに、友人と再会した。  
> On a visit to Kyoto, I reunited with a friend.
>
> 入学のさいに、案内の冊子を受け取った。  
> On enrolling, I received an information booklet.

In these examples, **とき**, **おり** and **さい** can all express the occasion, with differences in register and emphasis.
Do not make them competing quiz answers merely because one sounds more formal. Nor does さい mean an event can only
happen once. An occasion expression can appear in an account of habits; the distinction is that **たび itself explicitly
marks each occurrence**, while とき／おり／さい need not quantify occurrences that way.
**ついでに** adds a different relationship: doing a secondary activity in connection with a main one, not simply locating
something on an occasion.

### Causes, circumstances, and relationships

| Word | Central idea in these uses | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **[ため](../words/ため.md)** (tame) | Purpose or cause | 留学するために貯金している。 - I am saving to study abroad. |
| **[おかげ](../words/おかげ.md)** (okage) | A cause credited with a favorable result; also used ironically | 先生のおかげで合格できた。 - Thanks to my teacher, I was able to pass. |
| **せい** (sei) | A cause blamed for an unwanted result | 雨のせいで試合が中止になった。 - The game was canceled because of the rain. |
| **かわり** (kawari) | Substitution; other constructions express compensation or a trade-off | 私のかわりに彼が行った。 - He went instead of me. |
| **ついで** (tsuide) | Using one activity as an opportunity for another | 買い物のついでに、郵便局に寄った。 - While out shopping, I stopped by the post office. |
| **[かぎり](../words/かぎり.md)** (kagiri) | Limit, scope, or a continuing condition | 私が知るかぎり - as far as I know |
| **[ばあい](../words/場合.md)** (baai) | A case, situation, or condition | 雨のばあいは中止です。 - It will be canceled if it rains. |
| **うえ** (ue) | A relationship between events: here, completing a step before acting | 内容を確認したうえで、申し込んだ。 - I checked the details before applying. |
| **もと** (moto) | Being under guidance, authority, or conditions | 先生のもとで研究している。 - I am doing research under my teacher's guidance. |
| **[ほか](../words/ほか.md)** (hoka) | Something additional or outside a stated set | 英語のほかに、日本語も話せる。 - Besides English, I can also speak Japanese. |
| **わり** (wari) | An outcome judged against an expectation or proportion | この店は安いわりにおいしい。 - This restaurant's food is good considering how inexpensive it is. |
| **すえ** (sue) | An eventual outcome after a process | 何度も話し合ったすえに、決めた。 - After repeated discussions, we reached a decision. |
| **あげく** (ageku) | An eventual outcome, usually involving an unwelcome result or a troublesome process | 迷ったあげく、何も買わなかった。 - After much indecision, I bought nothing. |

Learn the connectors with the expression: **先生のおかげで**, **静かなわりに**, **確認したうえで**. For the outcome uses of **すえ** and **あげく**, common patterns are **た-form verb + すえ（に）／あげく（に）** and **noun + の + すえ（に）／あげく（に）**. Do not extend the まえ／あと rule to every time-related entry.

The selected use of **うえ** is not its only grammatical one: **安いうえにおいしい**, "cheap and tasty as well," adds a fact rather than sequencing events. Likewise, the **もと** here can be written **下／許**; it is not **元**, "former."

### とおり and もと: a model to follow or a context to act under

**とおり** connects an action or result to a model, instruction, expectation or plan that it matches.
**もと** in **のもとで** instead identifies guidance, supervision or conditions under which something takes place:

| Example | Relationship |
| --- | --- |
| 説明書のとおりに、部品を並べた。 | I arranged the parts as the instructions specified: the instructions supply the model. |
| 予定どおりに、九時に出発した。 | I left at nine as planned: the result matches the plan. |
| 先生のもとで、発音を研究している。 | I am researching pronunciation under my teacher's guidance: the teacher supervises the work. |
| 同じ条件のもとで、二つの材料を比べた。 | I compared two materials under the same conditions: the conditions apply to the comparison. |

Learn the connection as well as the meaning. After a noun, **予定どおり** and **予定のとおり** are both possible.
The direct compound uses voiced **どおり**; after **の**, keep **とおり**. Both are written **通り**.
A verb clause connects directly: **私が説明したとおりに**, "as I explained," and
**私が言うとおりに**, "as I tell you." Do not insert の or voice と after these verbs.

In **先生のもとで**, the noun connects through **の**. This **もと** can be written **下／許**;
recognizing both spellings does not mean that they are equally usual in every expression.
It does not imply that the researcher imitates the teacher's voice, nor is the conditions use limited to a human supervisor.
Learn this whole construction separately from **をもとに**, "using something as a basis," and **元**, "former."

Conformity expressions can overlap: **言われたとおりに**, **言われたように** and **言われたままに**
can all relate an action to what someone said, with context-dependent differences in emphasis.
Do not make them competing answers solely because one names "conformity" more explicitly.
The model/context exercises specify their noun list and supply the final に or で; they are controlled practice,
not a claim that these are the only possible ways to describe following instructions or receiving guidance.

### うえに and うえで: add a fact or establish a preliminary step

| Example | Relationship |
| --- | --- |
| この店は安いうえに、量も多い。 | The shop is inexpensive and also serves generous portions: a second fact is added. |
| 内装を新しくしたうえに、営業時間も延ばした。 | The shop renovated its interior and also extended its opening hours: two changes are added together. |
| 内容を確認したうえで、署名してください。 | Check the contents before signing: the check prepares for the signature. |
| 家族と相談したうえで、明日決めます。 | I will decide tomorrow after consulting my family: consultation comes before the decision. |

For additive **うえに**, verbs and i-adjectives connect directly, as in **新しくしたうえに** and **安いうえに**.
Use **な** after a na-adjective, as in **静かなうえに**, and **の** after a noun, as in
**学生のうえに**, "besides being a student." に can be omitted in additive **うえ、...**;
that does not make で an equivalent additive ending.

For the preliminary-step **うえで**, the basic connections are **た-form verb + うえで** and
**event noun + のうえで**, as in **十分な検討（けんとう）のうえで**, "after thorough consideration."
The first action is completed relative to the next one, not necessarily relative to now.
Consultation can still be in the future in **相談したうえで、明日決めます**.
This pattern often appears in instructions or fairly formal explanations of a procedure.

A past verb before うえ does **not** by itself choose between に and で: compare the second and third examples.
Use the relationship being expressed. Adding two actions does not itself specify their order or make one a prerequisite.
Conversely, preliminary-step うえで is not the whole inventory of うえで uses; do not extend this selected pattern's
attachment chart to every expression translated as "in doing" or "when considering."
The quiz's particle blanks explicitly request に or で and state the intended relationship.

### ため, おかげ and せい: a goal, a cause, or an evaluation

**ために** can introduce an intended goal or a cause of a result:

| Example | Relationship |
| --- | --- |
| 新しいプリンターを買うために、お金を貯めている。 | I am saving to buy a new printer: buying is the goal. |
| 新しいプリンターを買ったために、貯金が減った。 | My savings fell because I bought a new printer: the purchase is the cause. |

In the basic verb-goal pattern, use **dictionary-form verb + ために**, even with a past main clause:
**研修に参加するために、ホテルを予約した**, "I booked a hotel in order to attend the training."
With a noun, use **の**, as in **家族のために**, "for my family."

Do not turn the first table into a rule that every nonpast form expresses purpose and every cause needs a past verb.
**家賃が高いために借りられない**, "I cannot rent it because the rent is high," gives a cause with an i-adjective.
**周りが静かなために、よく眠れる**, "I sleep well because the surroundings are quiet," has a na-adjective and a favorable result.
The connectors are **高いため**, **静かなため** and **雨のため**. Causal ため can also occur without に.

**おかげで** normally credits a cause for a welcome result; **せいで** normally blames one for an unwanted result.
Neither word classifies the cause itself as inherently good or bad:

> 雨のおかげで、庭の花が元気になった。  
> Thanks to the rain, the garden flowers recovered.
>
> 雨のせいで、コンサートが中止になった。  
> The rain caused the concert to be canceled.

おかげ can also be ironic, much like sarcastic "thanks to" in English. Tone and context matter; a negative outcome does not
make every おかげ sentence impossible. ため often reports a cause without the same explicit credit or blame.
For attachment, compare **先生のおかげで**, **静かなおかげで**, **うるさいせいで** and **鍵を忘れたせいで**.
Do not add の after a completed plain verb clause such as 鍵を忘れた.

### かわり and ついで: replacement, an additional errand, or a trade-off

**かわりに** can express doing something instead, or acting in someone else's place.
**ついでに** uses a main activity as the occasion for another, secondary task:

| Example | Relationship |
| --- | --- |
| 電話のかわりに、メールを送った。 | I sent an email instead of phoning: one method replaces another. |
| 田中さんのかわりに、会議に出た。 | I attended the meeting in Tanaka's place. |
| 買い物のついでに、郵便局に寄った。 | I stopped at the post office while out shopping: shopping supplies the occasion. |
| 本を返したついでに、利用カードを更新した。 | On the occasion of returning books, I also renewed my library card. |

Use **の** after these event or person nouns. Verb phrases attach directly; ついで can follow a dictionary or past verb form,
as in **散歩するついでに**, "while out for a walk," or **本を返したついでに**, "on the occasion of returning the books."
The two actions need not happen at the same instant, and an additional errand need not replace the main one.

かわり also has exchange and compensating-contrast uses:

> 手伝うかわりに、夕食をごちそうしてもらう。  
> I will help in exchange for being treated to dinner.
>
> この宿は静かなかわりに、駅から遠い。  
> This inn is quiet, but the trade-off is that it is far from the station.

The second sentence weighs two features rather than replacing quietness or claiming that quietness causes the distance.
A drawback can also come first, as in **家賃が高いかわりに、会社に近い**, "The rent is high, but in return it is close to work."
Compare **高いかわりに** with **静かなかわりに**; use な after a na-adjective.
These are not all instances of the same "instead of" translation, and かわり is not a universal replacement for neutral "but."

### わりに and くせに: an expected standard or a reproachful contrast

**わりに** assesses something against an expectation suggested by the preceding description.
The result can be favorable or unfavorable:

> この車は小さいわりに、荷物がたくさん入る。  
> This car holds a lot of luggage for its small size.
>
> 練習したわりに、うまくできなかった。  
> I did not do very well considering the practice I had put in.

**くせに** conventionally puts reproach, dissatisfaction or a mocking edge into the contrast:

> 答えを知っているくせに、知らないふりをしている。  
> You know the answer, yet you are pretending not to know.

This is not simply an interchangeable neutral "although." The speaker's attitude is part of how the construction is used.
However, **わりに** and **のに** can also occur in critical sentences, and **くせに** can occur in teasing praise.
Anger or a good result alone is therefore not a reliable one-word answer key.
The quiz makes its frame explicit: an unmocking assessment against an expected standard, or the construction that
conventionally marks reproach itself. That is a controlled wording task, not unrestricted proof that another sentence
could never express a similar attitude.

For attachment, compare **値段のわりに／責任者のくせに** after nouns,
**静かなわりに／好きなくせに** after na-adjectives, and **小さいわりに／弱いくせに** after i-adjectives.
Verb clauses attach directly: **初めて作ったわりに** and **答えを知っているくせに**.
わり can be written **割／割り**; くせ can be written **癖**. These spellings do not create different constructions.
Nor does choosing わりに guarantee that a remark about another person will sound tactful: the standard being assumed
can itself be unwelcome.

### すえ and あげく: the outcome of a process

**すえ** presents an outcome after going through a process, often involving time or effort.
**あげく** commonly presents an unwelcome outcome after time, effort or trouble:

| Example | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| 何度も話し合ったすえに、計画がまとまった。 | After repeated discussions, the plan took shape. |
| いろいろな方法を試したすえに、修理をあきらめた。 | After trying various methods, I gave up the repair. |
| いろいろな方法を試したあげくに、修理をあきらめた。 | After all those attempts, I ended up giving up the repair. |

**すえ is not positive-only.** Both すえ and あげく fit the unsuccessful repair.
And **あげく is not an absolute ban on a favorable final result**: the trouble or negative evaluation can concern
the process of reaching it. Do not force these words into a good-result/bad-result opposition.

For the completed-process patterns here, use **た-form verb + すえ（に）／あげく（に）** or
**event noun + のすえ（に）／のあげく（に）**, as in **長い話し合いのすえに**.
The six eventual-outcome exercises all accept **すえ／あげく** in one answer class.
Three show すえ as the model option and three show あげく; this is shared practice, not six independent noun contrasts.
They cover difficult processes with disappointing outcomes, not every positive outcome or other use of either noun.
Spellings include **末** for すえ and **挙句／挙げ句／揚句／揚げ句** for あげく.

Distinguish that relationship from **うえに**, which adds a fact:
**事務所を移したうえに、設備も新しくした**, "We moved the office and also renewed its equipment."
The completed verb does not by itself turn the second improvement into the outcome of the move.
The quiz supplies に and explicitly asks for an additive or eventual-outcome relation; it does not reject every
alternative temporal formulation in a less restricted task.

### Construction-dependent classifications

| Item | Selected function | Example and classification caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **の** (no) | Nominalization | 本を読むのが好きだ。 - I like reading books. Some grammars call this a formal noun; others a nominalizing particle. See the discussion below. |
| **よう** (you) | Manner, resemblance, appearance, or a desired outcome | 忘れないようにメモした。 - I made a note so that I would not forget. Formal-noun analyses coexist with analyses treating **ようだ** and its forms as an auxiliary. |
| **くせ** (kuse), in **くせに** | A contrast carrying criticism or disapproval | 知っているくせに教えてくれない。 - You know, yet you won't tell me. Daijisen classifies **くせに** as a conjunctive particle; other dictionaries describe the noun-plus-particle combination. |

Do not treat **くせに** as a neutral replacement for "although." Nor does the noun origin of **よう** make every よう a noun: the ending in **食べよう**, "let's eat," is a different construction.

### ように and ような: comparisons and examples

For these uses, **ように** applies a comparison to an action, property or clause; **ような** directly modifies a noun:

| Example | What the comparison describes |
| --- | --- |
| 紙を見本（みほん）のように折ってください。 | How to fold the paper: follow the model. |
| 今日は春のように暖かい。 | How warm today is: like spring. |
| 先生が書いたように書いてください。 | How to write: as the teacher wrote it. |
| 雲のような形のクッション | A cushion with a cloud-like shape. |
| りんごのような果物 | Fruit such as apples. |
| 人に料理を教えるような仕事 | Work such as teaching people to cook. |

A preceding noun takes **の**, as in **見本のように**; a plain verb clause attaches directly, as in **先生が書いたように**.
Do not add の after that verb. Like na-adjective patterns, よう takes **に** for adverbial modification and **な** for
direct noun modification. This is not a rule that に must be followed immediately by an action verb:
**春のように暖かい** has an adjective, and a comparison can also frame a larger clause.

The scope of the comparison matters even inside a noun phrase:
**春のように暖かい日**, "a day that is warm like spring," is also grammatical; に relates to **暖かい**.
Simply spotting a noun somewhere later in the sentence does not choose between に and な.
The questions explicitly request either action/property comparison or direct modification of the named noun.

Resemblance and exemplification are not identical. A cloud-shaped cushion is not literally a cloud, but apples really
are fruit and can be among the fruits actually used in **りんごのような果物を使う**.
ような does not always mean "similar to, but not actually one of these."
Following a model can also be described with **とおり** or, in suitable contexts, **まま**;
other formulations such as **みたい** can express resemblance. The quiz supplies よう and tests only に／な,
not a universal distinction between all those expressions.

### ように: working toward a desired state

In a purpose construction, the clause before **ように** describes the state or result that an action aims to bring about:

| Example | Desired result |
| --- | --- |
| 子供でも案内が読めるように、ふりがなを付けた。 | I added furigana so that even children could read the notice. |
| 他の人が手紙を読めないように、封筒に入れて封をした。 | I sealed the letter in an envelope so that other people could not read it. |

The potential forms are **読む → 読める／読めない**, **使う → 使える／使えない** and
**入る（はいる） → 入れる（はいれる）／入れない（はいれない）**.
Here 入れる is the potential of "enter," not the separate **入れる（いれる）**, "put in."
The exercises explicitly request these potential forms ending in る or ない before printed ように.
They do not test every purpose pattern: **忘れないように**, "so as not to forget," has an ordinary negative verb.

The goal stays nonpast in **読めるように、ふりがなを付けた**, even though the preparation is past.
Preparation can also be planned for the future, and its actor need not be the person whose ability it is meant to affect.
A negative ability can be the wanted result, as when protecting a private letter.
Stating the purpose does not guarantee its achievement.

Neither に nor a potential/negative verb alone makes every よう expression a purpose.
Read how the clause relates to the rest of the sentence: a model, an example and a desired outcome are different relationships.
Other purpose formulations, including suitable **ために** sentences, are not globally ruled out by this controlled task.
Changes with **ようになる** and efforts with **ようにする** are treated next; indirect instructions and sentence-final wishes remain outside these exercises.

### ようになる and ようにする: a change or a deliberate practice

**ようになる** describes a change in what happens or what is possible. It is not limited to potential verbs:

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Potential verb + ようになる | 練習して、自転車に乗れるようになりました。 | After practicing, I became able to ride a bicycle. |
| Dictionary-form verb + ようになる | 今は通勤で毎日自転車に乗るようになりました。 | I now ride a bicycle every day for my commute. |
| Dictionary-form verb + ようにしている | 新しい道具を使う前には説明書を読むようにしています。 | I make a point of reading the instructions before using a new tool. |
| ない form + ようにしている | 休日は仕事のメールを読まないようにしています。 | I make a point of not reading work emails on days off. |

In **乗れるようになりました**, the newly available ability is the focus; in **乗るようになりました**, it is the
changed behavior. Someone who could already ride can begin commuting by bicycle.
The potential forms used in practice are **読む → 読める**, **乗る → 乗れる** and **話す → 話せる**.
These verb forms stay nonpast before よう even when **なりました** reports a completed change.

A change need not happen without deliberate action. Enlarging a screen's text can make it readable without glasses,
and repeated practice can develop a skill. Conversely, **ようになる** need not describe acquiring a skill:
the changed circumstances or the new routine may be what matters.
Do not treat なる as proof that nobody tried or する as proof that no change occurred.

In the effort use, **Vる／Vない＋ようにする** means making an effort or taking care to do or not do something.
**ようにしている** presents that care as a maintained practice, rather than simply the original decision to begin.
**読むことにした**, "I decided to read," can describe a related decision; it does not foreground the same ongoing care.
Both descriptions can apply to one person's routine without being interchangeable in every context.
Other **ようにする** constructions concern arranging a desired result, so the effort pattern is not its only use.

**読まないようにしている** is an effort **not to read**, not an absence of effort.
Likewise, **眠る前はスマートフォンを使わないようにしていますが、たまに見てしまいます。**
"I try not to use my smartphone before sleep, but I occasionally end up looking at it."
The effort expression does not guarantee success every time.
For an ability or habit that stops, a common basic pattern is instead **Vなくなる**:
**自転車に乗れなくなった**, "I became unable to ride a bicycle." That loss is not the same as deliberately avoiding cycling.

The first six questions supply **ようになりました** and request a dictionary or potential verb form.
They explicitly ask whether to report a new possibility or a new habit; the same three verbs appear in both directions.
The next six supply **ようにしています** and request a dictionary or ない form, contrasting doing with avoiding.
They practice the meaning and attachment of these supplied patterns, not unrestricted selection of **なる** versus **する**.
Other appropriate formulations of change, intention or effort remain possible outside those visible form restrictions.

### まま: a state remains unchanged

The preceding expression identifies the state that continues:

| Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 窓を開けたまま寝た。 | I slept with the window left open: the resulting state continued. |
| 食べないまま出かけた。 | I left without eating: the action remained undone. |
| この部屋は昔のままだ。 | This room is just as it used to be: its previous state is unchanged. |

The past form in 開けたまま describes the result of opening the window, not simply an action happening at the same time as sleeping.

For these basic patterns, compare **た-form + まま**, retaining the result of an action, with **ない-form + まま**, leaving the
action undone. The verb's meaning determines which state continues:

| With the action's result retained | With the action left undone |
| --- | --- |
| 窓を開けたまま - with the window left open | 窓を開けないまま - without opening the window |
| 電気を消したまま - with the lights kept off | 電気を消さないまま - without turning the lights off |
| 荷物を持ったまま - still holding the luggage | 荷物を持たないまま - without taking or holding the luggage |

Notice the reversal in the second row: **消す（けす）** means "turn off," so 消したまま keeps the lights **off**.
The affirmative form does not automatically mean "on" or "open," and the negative form does not automatically mean "off" or "closed."
まま need not describe a mistake: keeping the lights off while watching a film can be intentional.

Keep the **ない-form** in the basic "without doing" pattern even when the main event is past:
**窓を開けないまま外出しました**, "I went out without opening the window."
Other whole-sentence formulations, such as **窓を開けずに外出しました**, can also express going out without opening it;
they are not additional verb forms to insert directly before a supplied まま.

Unlike **たところ**, the retained-state use of **たまま** does not say that the action has just happened.
And unlike an ordinary **ながら** expression for simultaneous activities, it need not keep the preceding action in progress:
the window stays open, rather than repeatedly being opened.

### かぎり: scope or a continuing condition

**私が知るかぎり** limits a claim to the speaker's knowledge. With a condition, かぎり instead means "as long as that condition holds":

> 生きているかぎり、学び続けたい。  
> I want to keep learning as long as I live.

A negative condition often corresponds to English "unless":

> 予約しないかぎり入れない。  
> You cannot get in unless you make a reservation.

The last example says that making a reservation is necessary; it does not by itself guarantee admission.

Keep these two kinds of limit separate:

| Example | What is limited? |
| --- | --- |
| 外から見たかぎりでは、箱に傷はない。 | As far as an outside inspection shows, the box has no damage: the claim is limited to that evidence. |
| この時計が動くかぎり、使い続ける。 | I will keep using this clock as long as it works: the commitment holds under a continuing condition. |

In the first example, unseen contents may still be damaged. A knowledge or observation limit is not a guarantee that
unexamined evidence would agree. Plain verb phrases attach directly: **知っているかぎり**, **調べたかぎり**.
For a noun predicate used as a condition, **学生であるかぎり**, "as long as one is a student," uses **である**,
not the ordinary noun-linking の of 学生のとき.

With **ないかぎり**, read the whole conditional and the main clause's polarity:
**参加費を払わないかぎり、道具を借りられない**, "You cannot borrow the equipment unless you pay the fee."
If a form is also required, paying alone is still not enough. Conversely, a negative かぎり condition does not require
a negative main clause: **雨が降らないかぎり、予定どおり散歩する**, "Unless it rains, I will take the walk as planned."

### ばあい: identify the case that a procedure applies to

**ばあい** names a case or situation rather than, by itself, limiting the evidence for a claim:

| Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Noun + のばあいは | 雨の場合は体育館に集まってください。 - If it rains, gather in the gym. |
| Plain verb + ばあいは | パスワードを忘れた場合は、再設定してください。 - If you have forgotten your password, reset it. |
| Na-adjective + なばあいは | 登録が必要な場合は、申込書を送ってください。 - If registration is required, send the application form. |
| I-adjective + ばあいは | 料金が高い場合は、別の方法を考えます。 - If the fee is high, I will consider another method. |

The past verb in 忘れた場合 describes the case in which forgetting has occurred; an instruction with that form does
not assert that every reader has already forgotten a password. とき can often give natural alternative wording.
Likewise, **必要なかぎり** can mean "as long as it is necessary," but that explicitly maintains a condition rather than
simply naming one branch of a procedure. These differences in framing do not make the expressions mutually exclusive
in every context.

### とき versus ころ

**とき** identifies a time or occasion. **ころ** frames it as an approximate time or period, often a period of life.

The distinction is **not simply "short time versus long time."** Both 学生のとき and 学生のころ can refer to your student years as a whole. ころ makes the temporal boundaries less exact and often suits reminiscence; とき marks the relevant time without necessarily making it brief or precise.

| Example | Emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| 駅に着いたとき、雨が降り出した。 | It started raining when I arrived at the station. |
| 駅に着いたころ、雨が降り出した。 | It started raining around the time I arrived at the station. |

**ごろ** is the voiced suffix form used after time expressions, as in **三時ごろ** ("around three o'clock"). In **子供のころ**, ころ is a noun. Related meaning does not guarantee identical grammatical classification.

### A particularly important neighbor: の

の is another major nominalizer:

> 本を読むのが好きです。  
> I like reading books.

It often overlaps with こと, but not everywhere:

> 彼が泳いでいるのを見た。  
> I saw him swimming.

For directly perceived events like this, の is used rather than こと. Conversely, fixed expressions such as **ことができる** do not simply allow こと to be replaced by の.

Some teaching grammars include nominalizing の among 形式名詞; other descriptions classify it as a **準体助詞（じゅんたいじょし, nominalizing particle）**. This concerns the nominalizing use, not every function of の.

For learning purposes, **こと versus の** is the closest comparison for nominalization. **こと versus わけ** is more about content itself versus its explanatory interpretation.

### の, ん, なの, and なん

In explanatory **のだ／のです**, の commonly contracts to **ん**: **んだ／んです**. These constructions supply an explanation, background information, or a realization, rather than merely naming an action:

> 今日は行けないんです。  
> The thing is, I cannot go today.

The contraction is normal in speech, including polite んです. It is not a new formal noun, nor a rule allowing every の to become ん: **読むのが好き** is neutral standard Japanese, while 読むんが好き is dialectal or otherwise marked.

**なの and なん are combinations.** In the affirmative nonpast, nouns and na-adjectives need な before explanatory の; verbs and i-adjectives do not:

| Basic statement | With のだ | Contracted form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 行く - "[I] go" | 行くのだ | 行くんだ |
| 高い - "It is expensive" | 高いのだ | 高いんだ |
| 静かだ - "It is quiet" | 静かなのだ | 静かなんだ |
| 学生だ - "[I am] a student" | 学生なのだ | 学生なんだ |

Thus, **学生なんだ = 学生 + な + ん + だ**, with ん representing の. The same な + の sequence appears in **学生なの？** ("Are you a student?"); its polite explanatory counterpart is **学生なんですか？** Past forms work differently: **学生だったんです**, not 学生だったなんです.

Do not confuse this なん with **何（なん）**, "what": **これは何だ？** means "What is this?"

**もの contracts to もん**, not normally to a lone ん: **食べるもの → 食べるもん**, "something to eat." Sentence-final もの／もん can also express an emotionally emphasized reason or justification:

> だって、怖いんだもん。  
> But I am scared!

Here, **ん = の** and **もん = もの** occur together. Sentence-final もん is very casual and may sound insistent or childlike. For the family list, group ん with の and もん with もの, while learning the complete constructions and their different functions.

### もの／もん versus わけだ: a personal reason or a conclusion

Compare the speaker's purpose in these exchanges:

| Example | What the speaker is doing |
| --- | --- |
| 「もう寝るの？」「だって、眠いもん。」 | "Are you going to bed already?" "Because I am sleepy!" — offering a casual personal reason. |
| 三時間しか寝なかったの？　それなら、眠いわけだ。 | "You only slept three hours? No wonder you are sleepy." — reaching an explanatory understanding. |

In the first exchange, **もの** is also possible instead of **もん**. These sentence-final reason forms are casual and
can sound emotional or insistent; もん can also sound childlike. They are not default formal explanations.
**初めてなんだもん**, "because this is my first time," combines the already explained な＋ん＋だ with もん from もの.

Both kinds of sentence can relate to reasons. This comparison does not mean that わけ can never justify something,
or that every もの is an excuse. The exercises explicitly request either the casual personal-reason ending or a resulting
explanatory conclusion, and supply three exact endings in hiragana.
Natural alternatives such as **から**, longer **んだもの／んだもん** endings, or different conclusion expressions are not
made globally wrong by that restricted task.

## Particle neighbors: ほど, くらい／ぐらい, だけ, ばかり, and きり

These are worth recognizing alongside noun-based grammar, but **the uses in the tables below are classified as particles, not simply as formal nouns**. Noun-like behavior in one construction, or a noun origin, does not settle a word's present-day classification in another.

### ほど: degree, extent, and comparison

**[程](../kanji/程.md)（ほど）** has noun uses meaning "degree" or "extent": **実力のほど**, "the extent of someone's ability." Daijisen separates these from its **副助詞（ふくじょし, adverbial particle）** entry, which covers the following uses:

| Use | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approximate quantity | 10分ほど待った。 | I waited about ten minutes. |
| Degree illustrated by a situation | 二人は驚くほど似ている。 | The two are astonishingly alike. |
| Comparison with a negative | 今日は昨日ほど寒くない。 | Today is not as cold as yesterday. |
| Proportional change | 読めば読むほど面白くなる。 | The more I read, the more interesting it gets. |

Notice the actual patterns: **quantity + ほど**, **昨日 + ほど + a negative predicate**, and **読めば + 読むほど**. The last means "the more ..., the more ..."; ほど alone does not supply that entire meaning. These are not instances of a universal **noun + の + formal noun** attachment rule.

### Other useful particle neighbors

| Item | Selected function | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **[くらい／ぐらい](../words/くらい.md)** (kurai/gurai) | Approximate amount or degree | 10分くらい待った。 - I waited about ten minutes. |
| **[だけ](../words/だけ.md)** (dake) | Limitation; other uses express extent | 一つだけ選んだ。 - I chose just one. |
| **[ばかり](../words/ばかり.md)** (bakari) | Concentration on one thing or activity; other uses include approximation | 甘いものばかり食べている。 - You keep eating nothing but sweet things. |

**た-form verb + ばかり** has a different use: **食べたばかりだ**, "I have just eaten." It presents the event as recent from the speaker's perspective, not as repeated eating. Similar meanings do not make ほど, くらい, だけ, and ばかり interchangeable in every construction.

### Practicing quantities and action patterns

An estimate and an exclusive limit make different claims:

| Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| バスを三十分ほど待ちました。 | I waited about thirty minutes for the bus. |
| 休憩は五分だけです。 | The break is only five minutes. |

In the estimated-duration example, **くらい／ぐらい** and **ばかり** can also follow 三十分.
The quantity questions therefore group these natural alternatives in one answer class, rather than mark one wrong for not
being the model button. Their dictionary spellings **程／位／許り／許** are accepted too; kana is normal, especially for ばかり.
The tasks request uncontracted forms, not colloquial variants such as ばっかり.

The three exclusive-limit questions explicitly name only **ほど／くらい／ぐらい／だけ** as candidates.
They are a controlled contrast, not a claim that ばかり can never mean "only," or that だけ is the sole way to express a limit.
The spelling **丈（だけ）** is accepted, although kana is usual.
Do not transfer the quantity overlap to every degree, comparison or proportional-change pattern.

Before **ばかり**, the verb form changes the construction:

| Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 好きな小説を読んでばかりです。 | I am spending my time doing little but reading novels I enjoy. |
| この小説は昨日、最後まで読んだばかりです。 | I only just finished this novel yesterday. |

**てばかりです** can describe concentration on an activity, as **てばかりいます** does; いる is not obligatory in the first
version. This often occurs in complaints, but a speaker can also describe a happily chosen activity.
**たばかりです** presents an action as recently completed. "Recent" is relative to the situation and speaker, not a fixed
seconds-or-minutes cutoff. The questions supply ばかりです and request only the preceding verb's て or た form.
An alternative whole construction such as **たところです** is not globally wrong merely because it is outside that blank.

### Degree, comparison and desired extent

The words around **ほど** determine the relationship:

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A degree illustrated by a situation | 一人では持ち上げられないほど重い。 | So heavy that I cannot lift it alone. |
| Below a comparison standard | 青い箱は赤い箱ほど重くない。 | The blue box is not as heavy as the red one. |
| Correlated change | 考えれば考えるほど分からなくなる。 | The more I think about it, the less I understand it. |
| Desired extent with だけ | 使いたいだけ使ってください。 | Use as much as you want. |

In **持ち上げられないほど重い**, the negative describes the inability to lift, while **重い** is affirmative.
In **赤い箱ほど重くない**, the negative main adjective places the box below the comparison standard.
"Not as heavy" does not mean weightless, just as "not as expensive" does not necessarily mean cheap.
These degree examples describe stated consequences; other ほど descriptions can be figurative rather than literal.
The adjective questions supply ほど and です and request the standard plain nonpast form without extra particles.
Emphatic forms such as **高くはない** remain natural outside that explicit restriction.

**Vば＋Vる＋ほど** repeats the same verb: **読めば読むほど**, **考えれば考えるほど**, **使えば使うほど**.
It relates changes in degree; it does not promise improvement, as the growing-confusion example shows.
**Vたい＋だけ** instead lets the desired amount set the extent: **読みたいだけ**, **考えたいだけ**, **使いたいだけ**.
Form たい with the verb's ます stem. This amount need not be small.

The full-pattern questions explicitly request one of those two constructions, with the same supplied verb throughout.
Kana and kanji spellings are accepted, including **程** and **丈**, though kana is normal for these particles.
This is not a global rule separating the words: **読めば読むだけ** can also express correlated change,
and shorter **読むほど** or other extent formulations can be natural in appropriate sentences.
Likewise, **くらい／ぐらい** can overlap with clause-based degree without replacing ほど in every comparison or repeated-verb pattern.

### きり: only this set, or no further action since an event

**きり** is another useful particle neighbor, not automatically a formal noun because it is related to **切る**.
Daijisen gives it a separate particle entry. Other dictionaries divide some uses between particle and suffix entries;
learn the whole construction rather than assume that the classifications must match.

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A restricted occasion | チャンスは一度きりです。 | You have only one chance. |
| One person, without anyone else | 一人きりで夕食を作ります。 | I will make dinner alone. |
| Only the two participants | 二人きりで相談しました。 | Just the two of us discussed the matter. |
| A last event with no follow-up so far | 兄はその本を借りたきり、まだ返してくれません。 | My older brother borrowed the book and has not returned it since. |
| A follow-up on each occasion | 兄は本を借りるたびに、翌日には返してくれます。 | Every time my older brother borrows a book, he returns it by the next day. |

The first three examples do **not** have only one valid wording. **一度だけ／一度のみ／一度限り（いちどかぎり）**
can also express the one-chance limit. **一人だけで／二人だけで**, and the more formal **一人のみで／二人のみで**,
fit the person-limit examples too. **のみ** is more formal, not automatically incorrect.
Being **一人きり** need not mean feeling lonely, and **二人きり** need not describe a romantic situation.
These overlaps are contextual: **かぎり**, **だけ** and **のみ** are not interchangeable across all their other constructions.

The limit-versus-estimate questions name the particle families and request uncontracted forms.
They accept the suitable limit alternatives together, not as separate meanings to distinguish.
The estimates accept **ほど／くらい／ぐらい／ばかり** together; for example, **二十人ほどいました** gives an
approximate count rather than an exclusive group. This is not a universal ban on other approximation expressions.

**Vた＋きり** can mark the last completed event before a period with no further action, often specified by a negative clause.
In the book example, the missing follow-up is **returning** the book, not borrowing it again.
The reference time matters: **今朝出かけたきり、昼になってもまだ戻っていません。** means
"They went out this morning and still have not returned, even though it is now noon."
The person may still come home tonight. Likewise, **先週電話したきり、昨日まで連絡をくれませんでした。
でも、今日はまた電話がありました。** means "They called me last week and did not contact me again through yesterday.
But today they called again." This describes a gap that has now ended.
It does not claim the earlier call was the first ever or the final call for all time.
Do not turn this into a rule that きり always describes permanent consequences.

The temporal questions request either **Vた＋きり** or **Vる＋たびに**, including the verb in the blank:
**借りたきり／借りるたびに**, **出かけたきり／出かけるたびに**, **電話したきり／電話するたびに**.
The first identifies the completed event; the second means each occasion.
Other formulations can be natural: **借りたまま** describes an unchanged borrowing state, **借りて以来** introduces
a since-borrowing account, and **借りるごとに** can express every borrowing occasion. They are outside this visible
two-pattern attachment task, not globally wrong answers.

Colloquial **っきり** occurs in **一人っきり**, **二人っきり** and **借りたっきり**.
The temporal questions accept it; the earlier uncontracted-form questions deliberately do not.
Kana is usual for particle きり. Dictionaries also recognize **切り／限り／切／限**;
accepting those spellings is not recommending them as equally common or clear.
In particular, the spelling **限り** can represent **きり** as well as the separate **かぎり**:
do not infer from that spelling that **二人かぎり** is the ordinary way to say "just the two of us."
This practice does not cover every use, such as **寝たきり**, **これっきり**, or negative-predicate limitation.
