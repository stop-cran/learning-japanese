# Warmth, shine and surfaces in sentences

These exercises extend [Onomatopoeia in sentences](onomatopoeia-in-sentences.md) with descriptions of warmth, light and touch. The word is supplied: your job is to fit its selected meaning into the scene, not guess an English translation from a disconnected list. The individual cards explain additional senses; this guide does not claim that every sense uses every construction.

## Read what surrounds the blank

| What the scene does | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Describes a subject's own condition | 手が[ぽかぽか](../words/ぽかぽか.md)している。 | My hands feel pleasantly warm. |
| Describes how an action proceeds | ビーズを[ざらざら](../words/ざらざら.md)と出した。 | I poured out the beads with a rattle. |
| Attaches a characteristic to a noun | [ふかふか](../words/ふかふか.md)のパンをちぎった。 | I tore up the soft, cushiony bread. |
| States a condition with a copula | 道が[でこぼこ](../words/でこぼこ.md)です。 | The road is uneven. |
| Describes a change | 布団が[ほかほか](../words/ほかほか.md)になった。 | The bedding became lovely and warm. |
| Names a substance as a noun | 指の[ねばねば](../words/ねばねば.md)を洗い流した。 | I washed the sticky material off my fingers. |

Keep the printed verb or copula. Adding `に` before `する` is not a harmless spelling variant: compare **表面がつるつるしている** (the surface itself is smooth) with **表面をつるつるにしている** (someone is making or keeping the surface smooth). Their roles differ. The exercises about the subject's own condition do not ask for a different action with an unmentioned object.

With many of these words, both direct and `と` forms fit a supplied verb: **ざらざら出す／ざらざらと出す**. That is not permission to append `と` everywhere. Before a supplied `です`, for example, use **でこぼこです**, not *でこぼことです* or *でこぼこなです*.

## Warmth is not one interchangeable meaning

[ぽかぽか](../words/ぽかぽか.md) often describes pleasant warmth, but **ぽかぽかとたたく** depicts repeated thumps. The verb selects that different use; it does not say the object gets warm.

[ほかほか](../words/ほかほか.md) suits inviting warmth in food, towels or bedding. [ほくほく](../words/ほくほく.md) can describe a fluffy cooked interior, not merely high temperature. With a person pleased about good fortune, **ほくほくしている** instead describes satisfaction.

[あつあつ](../words/あつあつ.md) has useful state/modifier patterns such as **あつあつのスープ**. Do not invent a `する` form just because another mimetic takes one. With a couple, **二人はあつあつだ** means they are very much in love, not that someone measured their temperature.

[ひんやり](../words/ひんやり.md) describes felt coolness. **ひんやりした空気（くうき）** means cool-feeling air: this characteristic use of `した` is not automatically a report that the air used to be cool and no longer is.

## Noun modifiers belong to particular words

Several textures allow more than one short description:

- **ふかふかのパン／ふかふかなパン／ふかふかしたパン** describe cushiony-soft bread.
- **すべすべの肌（はだ）／すべすべした肌** describe skin's smooth feel.
- **もちもちした肌** describes supple, springy skin; translating it as “chewy skin” would be misleading.

The attachment exercises describe the noun's characteristic at the scene's reference time using short attribute connectors or nonpast descriptive forms, including ordinary `している` and `してる` where appropriate. Descriptive `した` also fits this property description. Retrospective past-state clauses such as `していた` or `だった` are outside these stated drills, not ungrammatical Japanese: **ぱさぱさしていたパンをスープに浸した** can describe bread that was dry before soaking. The task instead asks for its property at that pre-soaking reference point using the bounded forms.

A `である` modifier can be grammatical but more formal; accepted alternatives are not a claim that every form is equally frequent or stylistically identical. An explicitly labeled `した` formation drill asks for that particular transformation instead of every possible noun description.

Do not export this flexibility to all words. The pilot's dependable-person example is **しっかりした人／しっかりしている人**, not an ordinary *しっかりな人*. Likewise, a word's dictionary labels are not a recipe for attaching every suffix to every sense.

## Shine, softness and roughness have different focuses

[ぴかぴか](../words/ぴかぴか.md) describes bright shine, including a result of polishing. In **ぴかぴかの一年生**, it instead presents a brand-new pupil; literal reflected light is not required.

[ぎらぎら](../words/ぎらぎら.md) suggests a fierce glare. **ぎらぎらした目** can suggest intensity, but does not by itself identify a particular emotion or someone hated. [つやつや](../words/つやつや.md) focuses on gloss, while [てかてか](../words/てかてか.md) often suggests conspicuous or excessive shine. Neither gloss nor shine alone proves slipperiness, dirt or grease.

[ふかふか](../words/ふかふか.md) is thick cushioning; [もこもこ](../words/もこもこ.md) foregrounds bulky fluff or a bulging shape. Layered clothing can be もこもこ without being especially soft. [もちもち](../words/もちもち.md) emphasizes springiness, and [ぷにぷに](../words/ぷにぷに.md) soft yielding under light pressure. None automatically means wet, sticky or edible.

[つるつる](../words/つるつる.md) can foreground a smooth, slick surface; [すべすべ](../words/すべすべ.md) emphasizes smoothness under the hand. With **そばをつるつると食べる**, however, the eating verb selects slurping noodles, not skin texture or deliciousness.

[ざらざら](../words/ざらざら.md) describes fine roughness or grit. [ごつごつ](../words/ごつごつ.md) suggests larger hard irregularities, while [でこぼこ](../words/でこぼこ.md) concerns raised and sunken parts. **壁（かべ）のでこぼこを埋める（うめる）** uses でこぼこ as a noun, “fill in the uneven spots.” **でこぼこコンビ** is a mismatched pair; the mismatch need not mean they get along badly. The compound keeps the reading でこぼこ, not another reading of 凸凹.

## Sticky material, repeated contact and moisture

[べたべた](../words/べたべた.md) can describe a sticky surface, extensive pasting, or clinging to a person. Compare **机がべたべたする**, **広告（こうこく）をべたべた貼る（はる）**, and **母親にべたべたする**. A social description does not establish literal glue.

[ねばねば](../words/ねばねば.md) highlights viscosity and can name the sticky material itself. **ねばねばを洗い流す** therefore needs no extra `する`. The texture can be valued in food; visible stretched threads are a prototype, not a universal requirement.

[ぬるぬる](../words/ぬるぬる.md) often describes a slippery coating. In informal screen/game descriptions, **人物がぬるぬる動く／ぬるぬると動く** can instead praise fluid movement. This does not mean the display has slime on it, and the word is not a temperature term meaning ぬるい.

[ぺたぺた](../words/ぺたぺた.md) describes repeated flat contact: bare feet walking, a sponge daubing paint, or stickers being applied. The action does not automatically tell you the finished surface feels sticky.

[ぱさぱさ](../words/ぱさぱさ.md) describes dried-out food or hair, not necessarily a pleasant crunch. [しっとり](../words/しっとり.md) can describe gently moist cake, but **しっとりと歌う** selects a quiet, restrained delivery rather than literal wetness. Context decides whether moisture is welcome; the word does not make all dampness pleasant.

## Using the exercises

Follow the stated operation and use the supplied word, in hiragana or katakana. Fill the complete blank, not the entire sentence. In choice mode, select **any one** fitting answer if several are displayed. Interpretation questions use the fixed labels **言える** and **言えない** to separate what the wording supports from an added assumption.

The practice covers representative constructions for these 24 existing cards. It is not a frequency ranking, an exhaustive dictionary, a new JLPT classification or evidence that completing the set guarantees transfer to unseen Japanese.
