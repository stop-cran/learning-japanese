# Japanese onomatopoeia: sounds, textures, movements, and feelings

[ごろごろ](../words/ごろごろ.md) can describe rumbling thunder, a heavy object rolling, or
someone spending the day lazing around. [ぴかぴか](../words/ぴかぴか.md) describes gleaming
brightness, while [ぽかぽか](../words/ぽかぽか.md) often describes pleasant warmth.
Only some of these uses imitate an audible sound.

Japanese calls this broad family **オノマトペ**. English explanations also use *mimetic words*
or *ideophones* for vivid descriptions of states and sensations. They belong in ordinary
sentences, not just manga sound effects: a recipe may describe food as
[さくさく](../words/さくさく.md), and a weather report can describe rain or thunder.
Their suitability for formal writing depends on the word and context.

## Three useful labels, not three sealed boxes

| Label | Useful learner meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 擬音語（ぎおんご）, giongo | Sounds made by objects or nature | [ぐつぐつ](../words/ぐつぐつ.md): bubbling or simmering |
| 擬声語（ぎせいご）, giseigo | Human or animal voices | [くすくす](../words/くすくす.md): quiet giggling |
| 擬態語（ぎたいご）, gitaigo | A state, sensation, or manner rather than necessarily an audible sound | [ふわふわ](../words/ふわふわ.md): fluffy, light, or floating |

This is a simplified teaching classification. Some sources separate movements as
擬容語（ぎようご） and feelings as 擬情語（ぎじょうご）; here those fit under the broad
state/manner label. A word can belong to more than one category **in different uses**:
ごろごろ is not always a sound, and [どきどき](../words/どきどき.md) can evoke both a
pounding heart and nervous excitement. Category tags are study aids, not grammatical parts of speech.

## Learn a scene and a construction together

Instead of memorizing only "ぽかぽか = warm," learn a short phrase and its situation:

- 日なたで体がぽかぽかしてきた。 (ひなたで、からだがぽかぽかしてきた。) — My body started
  feeling pleasantly warm in the sun.
- 鍋の中でスープがぐつぐつ煮えている。 (なべのなかで、スープがぐつぐつにえている。) —
  The soup is bubbling away in the pot.
- 発表の前はどきどきする。 (はっぴょうのまえは、どきどきする。) — My heart races before
  giving a presentation.

These are original teaching examples, not quotations from manga or news reports.
For a word with several meanings, keep separate scenes: a thunderstorm does not teach the
"lazing around" sense of ごろごろ.

## Grammar: do not attach する or と to everything

For focused sentence practice, continue with [Onomatopoeia in sentences](onomatopoeia-in-sentences.md).
Its twelve-word core and focused companion sets supply familiar words and practise selected
constructions across the 203-card collection, separately from the vocabulary-card schedules.
This covers representative uses of each card, not every dictionary sense or possible attachment.

Common patterns include an adverb before a verb, an adverb with と, and a state expressed
with する. Some words also work with な or に. These patterns are **word-specific**:

| Pattern | Natural example | What to learn |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Word + verb | [ぐっすり](../words/ぐっすり.md)眠る（ねむる） — sleep soundly | Learn the verb together with the word. |
| Word + と + verb | [にっこり](../words/にっこり.md)と笑う（わらう） — give a smile | と can introduce manner; its availability is not universal. |
| Word + する | [わくわく](../words/わくわく.md)する — feel excited in anticipation | The whole expression is the predicate. |
| Word + な + noun | [ふかふか](../words/ふかふか.md)な布団（ふとん） — a soft, well-padded futon | Do not assume every word accepts な. |
| Word + に + change | [びしょびしょ](../words/びしょびしょ.md)になる — become soaking wet | に marks the resulting state. |

In particular, ぐっすり眠る does not license a general rule "*ぐっすりする = sleep."
Check the card's examples rather than building a new construction from an English gloss.

## Spelling and patterns

Both hiragana and katakana occur: ぴかぴか and ピカピカ do not automatically name different words.
Katakana can make an effect stand out visually, but "sound words always use katakana, state words
always use hiragana" is not a reliable rule. This collection normally uses one hiragana card per
word, rather than two review schedules for a spelling change.

Repeated forms are striking, but **not every word is repeated**: じっと, そっと, にっこり, and
ぐったり belong here too. Conversely, repetition alone does not make a word onomatopoeic:
人々（ひとびと）, "people," is not a sound effect.

Continue with [sound patterns and voicing](onomatopoeia-sound-patterns.md),
[textures and warmth](onomatopoeia-texture-and-warmth.md), or
[rain, movement, and similar-looking words](onomatopoeia-sound-and-movement.md).

The second batch adds guides to [eating and drinking](onomatopoeia-eating-and-drinking.md),
[body sensations and feelings](onomatopoeia-body-and-feelings.md), and

## Sources and limits

- [JMdict](https://www.edrdg.org/wiki/index.php/JMdict-EDICT_Dictionary_Project): the original batches identify
  their inspected entries. Later cards cite their own dictionary evidence, including Japanese dictionary entries.
  Dictionary senses support lexical meanings, not a claim that every word is equally frequent.
- [Tofugu: Japanese onomatopoeia](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese/japanese-onomatopoeia/):
  accessible introduction to the five-way classification, spelling, forms, and grammar.
  Its sound-symbolism generalizations are learning tendencies, not exceptionless rules.

The examples and contrasts here are original explanations. Named scene illustrations show selected uses;
they do not replace sentence patterns or establish a universal image-to-word translation. Some scenes are
article-only rather than quiz choices.
