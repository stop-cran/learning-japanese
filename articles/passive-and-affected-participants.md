# Passive voice and affected participants

Why can 招待された（しょうたいされた）, "was invited," describe a welcome event, while
ケーキを食べられた（たべられた） can mean someone ate **your** cake, not you?
Japanese passive **受け身（うけみ）** changes how an event is presented. It does not automatically
make the event unpleasant, and its subject need not be the thing directly acted on.

Start with [verb classes and ordinary inflection](japanese-verb-foundations.md) and
[noun roles](japanese-nouns-and-basic-particles.md#が-and-を-who-or-what-has-which-role).
Reading [potential forms](ability-and-perception.md#form-an-ordinary-potential-verb) first also helps:
some potential and passive forms look identical, but they do different jobs.

## Form the passive, then inflect it

| Verb class | Operation | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Godan | Change the final u-row kana to its a-row counterpart, then add れる; final う becomes わ | 読む → 読まれる（よまれる）; 呼ぶ → 呼ばれる（よばれる）; 買う → 買われる（かわれる） |
| Ichidan | Remove final る, then add られる | 褒める → 褒められる（ほめられる）; 見つける → 見つけられる（みつけられる） |
| する | Use される, retaining a preceding noun | 公開する → 公開される（こうかいされる）, be released to the public |
| 来る（くる） | Use 来られる, pronounced こられる | 来られた（こられた）, experienced someone's coming |

The godan changes are **う→わ, く→か, ぐ→が, す→さ, つ→た, ぬ→な, ぶ→ば, む→ま, る→ら**.
Thus 書く becomes 書かれる（かかれる） and 取る becomes 取られる（とられる）.
A verb ending in る is not automatically ichidan; check its class before choosing the operation.

The resulting passive conjugates like an **ichidan verb**, even when its original verb was godan:

| Time and polarity | Plain | Polite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Affirmative nonpast | 呼ばれる | 呼ばれます |
| Negative nonpast | 呼ばれない | 呼ばれません |
| Affirmative past | 呼ばれた | 呼ばれました |
| Negative past | 呼ばれなかった | 呼ばれませんでした |

The readings start with よばれ throughout. Form **呼ばれる** first, then replace its final る;
do not attach the passive ending to 呼んだ or conjugate the new verb as godan.
Plain/polite and affirmative/negative are separate choices.

> この映画は去年公開された。  
> このえいがは きょねん こうかいされた。  
> This film was released last year.
>
> この映画は去年公開されなかった。  
> This film was not released last year.

The negative denies the release in that time frame. It does not by itself tell us who decided against it,
why it did not happen, or whether the film was released later.

## Follow the participants, not just the English word order

> 先生が私を褒めた。  
> せんせいが わたしを ほめた。  
> The teacher praised me.
>
> 私は先生に褒められた。  
> わたしは せんせいに ほめられた。  
> I was praised by the teacher.

The teacher still does the praising. 私 is still the person praised. The second sentence makes my
experience the topic; 先生に identifies the actor in this passive frame.
Here the passive subject is the direct target of the original action: this is a straightforward
**direct passive**. An animal or thing can also be the target:

> 猫は妹に見つけられた。  
> ねこは いもうとに みつけられた。  
> The cat was found by my younger sister.

Do not memorize "が always marks the doer" or "に always means to." The predicate determines the frame.
は marks a topic and can stand where a subject would otherwise be marked by が; it is not a passive suffix.
Participants can be omitted when understood:

> 受付で名前を呼ばれた。  
> うけつけで なまえを よばれた。  
> My name was called at reception.

This naturally presents the experience of the person whose name was called. Japanese does not require
an explicit 私は or an explicitly named caller.

## A person can be affected while another object keeps を

Compare these two accounts:

> 私のケーキは弟に食べられた。  
> わたしのケーキは おとうとに たべられた。  
> My cake was eaten by my younger brother.
>
> 私は弟にケーキを食べられた。  
> わたしは おとうとに ケーキを たべられた。  
> My younger brother ate my cake, affecting me.

In the second sentence **I am not what gets eaten**. 弟 is the eater; ケーキを still marks what he eats.
私は introduces the affected person's viewpoint. It often suggests an unwelcome experience here,
but no passive ending mechanically encodes "angry," "deliberate," or "without permission."

The same distinction matters with a body part:

> 私は電車で隣の人に足を踏まれた。  
> わたしは でんしゃで となりのひとに あしを ふまれた。  
> The person next to me stepped on my foot on the train.

足を is the object of 踏む（ふむ）, "step on"; 私 is the affected person.
Possessive/body-part passives are classified differently in different descriptions of Japanese.
For practical reading, establish these roles first rather than relying on a single direct/indirect label.
It is not a rule that every active を must disappear or become が.

## Even an intransitive event can affect someone

An **intransitive** verb does not take an ordinary direct object in the event described.
Nevertheless, some intransitive events can be presented through an affected person's passive:

> 私は帰り道に雨に降られた。  
> わたしは かえりみちに あめに ふられた。  
> I got caught in the rain on the way home.
>
> 私は夜中に赤ちゃんに泣かれた。  
> わたしは よなかに あかちゃんに なかれた。  
> The baby cried in the middle of the night, affecting me.

Rain falls; the baby cries. There is no ordinary object being rained or cried.
The passive adds the person's affected experience rather than turning that person into an object
of the original verb. This kind of use is commonly called **indirect passive**.

An unexpected visit can work similarly:

> 私は仕事中に友達に来られた。  
> わたしは しごとちゅうに ともだちに こられた。  
> A friend came while I was working, putting me in an awkward situation.

Here 来られた is about another person's visit, not my ability to come.
These conventional examples do **not** license mechanically passivizing every intransitive verb
in every setting. Naturalness depends on the event and how it affects the person.

## Passive is not a universal complaint

> 私は友達の結婚式に招待された。  
> わたしは ともだちの けっこんしきに しょうたいされた。  
> I was invited to my friend's wedding.
>
> この家は去年建てられた。  
> このいえは きょねん たてられた。  
> This house was built last year.

The invitation may be welcome; the house sentence can simply report a fact.
Neither sentence establishes the speaker's feelings. Indirect affected-person frames often convey
inconvenience, but context still matters; do not transfer that tendency to every passive.

に is a useful starting point for an expressed actor. から can also mark a source/actor in appropriate
patterns, and formal reporting often uses によって. They are not freely interchangeable in all passive
sentences. The exercises here keep those markers supplied rather than pretending one particle rule
settles every verb and context.

## Shared られる: passive or potential?

| Verb | Passive | Standard potential |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 読む（よむ） | 読まれる（よまれる） | 読める（よめる） |
| 買う（かう） | 買われる（かわれる） | 買える（かえる） |
| 食べる（たべる） | 食べられる（たべられる） | 食べられる（たべられる） |
| 来る（くる） | 来られる（こられる） | 来られる（こられる） |
| する | される | できる |

Godan forms differ. Ichidan forms and 来る share the standard spelling and pronunciation:

> 歯が治ったので、私は固いパンも食べられる。  
> はが なおったので、わたしは かたいパンも たべられる。  
> My teeth have healed, so I can eat hard bread too.
>
> 私は弟にパンを食べられた。  
> My younger brother ate my bread, affecting me.

The first describes an available action; the second presents me as affected by someone else's action.
Neither the presence of は nor the spelling られる alone settles the meaning.
Read the participants, surrounding situation and whole predicate.
Potential past forms can describe an ability that was actually exercised, so "something happened"
is not by itself a passive test.

Conversational 食べれる and 来れる（これる） are potential forms with ら omitted.
Do not use that omission to form the passive. Respectful and spontaneous uses of れる／られる
are separate readings, not extra required answers in this lesson; this is not an exhaustive
classifier for every occurrence of られる.

## What the practice asks

The groups separate **formation**, **participant interpretation**, **sentence-frame interpretation**
and **passive/potential interpretation**. The frame questions use three explicitly defined response
codes: 直接（ちょくせつ） for a topic that itself receives the lexical action,
別対象（べつたいしょう） for an affected person with a different を-object, and
出来事（できごと） for an affected person in an intransitive event.
These are local answer codes, not a universal classification of possessive passives.

Named form conversions require changing the supplied verb, not copying a missing suffix.
Paired forms and repeated scenes are useful scaffolding, not evidence of independent conversational
mastery. The ordinary kanji/kana model answers do not imply that recognized alternative spellings
are equally common.

For a different distinction, see [actions, events and resulting states](actions-events-and-results.md):
an intransitive event such as 開く is not automatically a passive form of 開ける.
The causative and respectful roles of related verb endings are later topics, not prerequisites here.
