# Japanese listing and examples: と, や, とか, など, たり, and し

Japanese has several ways to say "A and B," "things like A and B," or "A, for one thing."
The choice depends on **what is being connected** and **how the speaker presents it**, not just how many
items appear. A single example can be enough, and two named items do not necessarily make a complete inventory.

Useful terms are **listing/enumeration**, **列挙（れっきょ）**, and **exemplification**, **例示（れいじ）**:
giving examples. Textbooks often contrast **exhaustive (closed)** and **non-exhaustive (open)** lists.
That is a useful starting point, but it is not a complete account of these constructions.

## First choose the kind of connection

| What you want to do | Main pattern | Starting interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Join named nouns or noun phrases | AとB | A and B |
| Give representative nouns | AやB, AとかB | A, B, and things like them |
| Mark something as an example | Nなど | N, for example / N and similar things |
| Give examples of activities or states | 〜たり（〜たり）する | do things like A; sometimes be A, sometimes B |
| Add facts or considerations | 〜し（〜し） | A, and B; A is one consideration |
| Connect predicates | 〜て, 〜くて, 〜で | A and B; the relationship depends on context |
| Emphasize inclusion | AもBも | both A and B |
| Present alternatives | AかB | A or B |
| Suggest one possible example | Nでも | N or something, in an appropriate suggestion |

Here **N** means a noun or noun phrase. A **predicate** says what happens or what something is like:
a verb, an adjective, or a noun with an ending such as だ or です.
The table is a map of selected uses, not a claim that each expression has only one meaning.

## と: join the named nouns

> 本とノートを買いました。  
> （ほんとノートをかいました。）  
> I bought books and notebooks.

**本とノート** forms a group, and **を** marks that whole group as the object of buying.
Longer lists work too: **本とノートとペン**, "books, notebooks, and pens."

This is the central contrast with **や**: と directly joins the named items rather than presenting them
with a dedicated example-list marker. If someone asks for a complete inventory, a と-list can supply it.
But **と is not itself an "only" particle**. The sentence above does not grammatically prohibit the speaker
from also having bought an umbrella.

If exclusion matters, make it explicit:

> 買ったのは本とノートだけです。ほかには何も買っていません。  
> （かったのはほんとノートだけです。ほかにはなにもかっていません。）  
> Books and notebooks are all I bought. I have not bought anything else.

Here **だけ**, "only," and the following sentence establish the limit. Do not use the mere presence of と
as proof that nothing else exists or happened.

### This と joins nouns, not arbitrary verb clauses

To name activities as things, nominalization is useful:

> 読むことと書くことは、どちらも大切です。  
> （よむこととかくことは、どちらもたいせつです。）  
> Reading and writing are both important.

The two **こと** expressions behave as noun phrases. See [noun-based grammar](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md)
for こと and の as nominalizers.

By contrast, **食べると眠くなる**, "when I eat, I get sleepy," uses a
[conditional と](japanese-conditionals.md), not noun-listing と.
Quotation と is another separate use.

Also distinguish a list from accompaniment:

| Sentence | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 先生と学生が来ました。 | A teacher and a student came. |
| 先生と来ました。 | I came with a teacher. |

**先生（せんせい）** means teacher; **学生（がくせい）** means student.
In the first sentence と connects the two named participants; in the second it marks the companion.

## や, とか, and など: examples rather than a complete inventory

### や: representative nouns

> 本やノートを買いました。  
> I bought things such as books and notebooks.

The items are presented as examples of the purchases, not as a complete inventory.
In this straightforward report, books and notebooks are things the speaker bought:
**や does not automatically mean "maybe one or the other."**

The ordinary finished noun-list pattern is **AやB**, with further nouns possible.
For "A, for example" with only one named item, **Aなど** or **Aとか** is a more useful starting pattern
than leaving an ordinary list hanging at **Aや**.

You can add など:

> 本やノートなどを買いました。  
> I bought books, notebooks, and similar things.

や already presents examples; など reinforces that framing. It is not obligatory after every や-list.

### とか: conversational examples, including activities

> 休みの日は、小説とか漫画とかを読みます。  
> （やすみのひは、しょうせつとかまんがとかをよみます。）  
> On days off, I read things like novels and manga.

**とか** is generally more conversational than や. Both **AとかB** and **AとかBとか** occur.
The second とか is not a requirement in every noun list.

One example also works:

> 週末は映画とかどうですか。  
> （しゅうまつはえいがとかどうですか。）  
> How about a movie or something this weekend?

The movie is one possible suggestion; no second suggestion has to be spoken.

Unlike ordinary noun-listing や, とか can also follow clauses:

> 週末は、映画を見るとか、散歩するとかしませんか。  
> （しゅうまつは、えいがをみるとか、さんぽするとかしませんか。）  
> This weekend, shall we do something like watch a movie or take a walk?

Here the examples are activities, expressed with **見る**, "watch," and **散歩する**, "take a walk."
This is a suggestion, not a report that both activities have already happened.
Do not infer actual events merely from an example list.

とか also has uncertainty and reporting uses. A とか after a clause is not automatically an enumeration;
the surrounding construction still matters.

### など: mark an example or a group of examples

> 旅行には地図などを持っていきます。  
> （りょこうにはちずなどをもっていきます。）  
> I take things such as maps on trips.

**地図など** is already a complete example phrase: "maps and similar things."
It does not need a second named item.
など can also follow a list, and it can help modify a category noun:

> りんごなどの果物を使います。  
> （りんごなどのくだものをつかいます。）  
> We use fruit such as apples.

The pattern is **Nなどの + category noun**. Do not omit の in this noun-modifying pattern.

など is not limited to nouns:

> 本を読むなどして、静かに過ごしました。  
> （ほんをよむなどして、しずかにすごしました。）  
> I spent the time quietly, doing things like reading.

This uses **dictionary-form verb + などして**. 読む does not place the reading in the future:
the whole sentence reports how the time was spent.

Importantly, **と and など can occur together**:

> 本とノートなどを買いました。  
> I bought books, notebooks, and things like that.

Spotting と inside an example phrase does not make the whole expression exhaustive.
The reach of など can depend on grouping and context; do not read a list by inspecting one particle in isolation.

Nor is every など a neutral English "etc." It can soften an expression or convey a dismissive attitude.
The nearby conversational forms **なんか** and **なんて** also have uses beyond neutral exemplification;
they are not automatic replacements in every sentence.

## たり: representative activities and variable states

> 日曜日は、料理をしたり、庭で野菜を育てたりします。  
> （にちようびは、りょうりをしたり、にわでやさいをそだてたりします。）  
> On Sundays, I do things like cook and grow vegetables in the garden.

This presents examples of how Sundays are spent, not a step-by-step account of everything done.
Listing cooking first does not itself say it happens before gardening.

### Form: the た-form shape plus り

For verbs, make the ordinary **た-form**, then add **り**. The following table also shows corresponding
adjective and noun-predicate forms:

| Starting expression | たり form | Meaning of the item |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 食べる | 食べたり | eating |
| 読む | 読んだり | reading |
| 泳ぐ | 泳いだり | swimming |
| する | したり | doing |
| 来る（くる） | 来たり（きたり） | coming |
| 行く（いく） | 行ったり（いったり） | going |
| 安い | 安かったり | being inexpensive |
| 静かだ | 静かだったり | being quiet |
| 休みだ | 休みだったり | being a day off / being on a break |
| 食べない | 食べなかったり | not eating |

Read **食べる（たべる）**, **読む（よむ）**, **泳ぐ（およぐ）**, **安い（やすい）**,
**静か（しずか）**, and **休み（やすみ）**.
Do not attach たり directly to every dictionary form: the basic forms are **読んだり**, not **読むたり**,
and **静かだったり**, not **静かたり**.

The basic complete pattern is **〜たりする**, with further たり items if needed.
The する part can be inflected or connected to another predicate:

| Sentence | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 休みの日は本を読んだりします。 | On days off, I do things like read. |
| 昨日は本を読んだりしました。 | Yesterday, I did things like read. |
| 明日は本を読んだりするつもりです。 | Tomorrow, I intend to do things like read. |
| 本を読んだりして過ごします。 | I spend the time doing things like reading. |

**昨日（きのう）** means yesterday; **明日（あした）** means tomorrow;
**過ごす（すごす）** means spend time.
The た-form shape inside たり **does not by itself mean that the activity happened in the past**.
The complete construction and its context distinguish a habit, a completed episode, and a plan.
In **たりして過ごす**, する appears as **して** rather than ending the sentence.

### One たり is possible

The first sentence in the table, **休みの日は本を読んだりします**, is not missing a compulsory second たり.
Reading is the stated example, and further activities are left unspecified.

This is different from accidentally marking only part of an intended two-item たり list.
For the straightforward pattern, keep the items parallel:
**本を読んだり、音楽を聞いたりします**, "I do things like read and listen to music."
Here **音楽（おんがく）** means music, and **聞く（きく）** means listen.
One genuine example and an inconsistently formed multi-item list are not the same issue.

### Alternation is not just an unspoken "etc."

> この時期は、暑かったり寒かったりします。  
> （このじきは、あつかったりさむかったりします。）  
> At this time of year, it alternates between being hot and cold.
>
> 朝ご飯は、食べたり食べなかったりします。  
> （あさごはんは、たべたりたべなかったりします。）  
> Sometimes I eat breakfast and sometimes I do not.

Contrasting states or actions can describe variation across occasions. There is no need to invent a third state
to complete an imaginary hidden list.

Notice where the negative belongs in **食べなかったり**: not eating is one of the alternating situations.
Negating the final する is different:

> 夜にコーヒーを飲んだりしません。  
> （よるにコーヒーをのんだりしません。）  
> I do not do things like drink coffee at night.

That denies doing the example activity; it is not an affirmative account of alternating between doing it and not doing it.

## し: add facts, considerations, or reasons

> この部屋は静かだし、机も広いし、勉強しやすいです。  
> （このへやはしずかだし、つくえもひろいし、べんきょうしやすいです。）  
> This room is quiet, and the desk is spacious too, so it is easy to study here.

The quiet room and spacious desk support an assessment.
し is useful when accumulating reasons, but it also connects facts without an explicit causal conclusion:

> 姉は英語が話せるし、兄は中国語が話せます。  
> （あねはえいごがはなせるし、あにはちゅうごくごがはなせます。）  
> My older sister can speak English, and my older brother can speak Chinese.

Neither ability causes the other. The facts are being added together.

### Attachment: use a predicate before し

| Predicate | Example | Meaning of the first part |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verb, nonpast | よく読むし | someone reads often, and ... |
| Verb, negative | お金もかからないし | it does not cost money either, and ... |
| Verb, past | もう読んだし | someone has already read it, and ... |
| い-adjective | 安いし | it is inexpensive, and ... |
| な-adjective, affirmative nonpast | 静かだし | it is quiet, and ... |
| Noun predicate, affirmative nonpast | 休みだし | it is a day off, and ... |
| Past noun or な-adjective predicate | 休みだったし／静かだったし | it was a day off / was quiet, and ... |
| Negative noun or な-adjective predicate | 休みではないし／静かじゃないし | it is not a day off / is not quiet, and ... |

**お金（おかね）がかかる** means cost money.
Use **だし** after an affirmative nonpast noun or な-adjective in these plain patterns.
It is **静かだし**, not **静かなし** or **静かのし**.
Compare **静かなのに**, "although it is quiet": the connector required by
[のに](noni-and-concessive-contrasts.md) is different.

Polite predicates can also precede し: **静かですし**, "it is quiet, and ...",
and **よく読みますし**, "I read often, and ...".
A plain predicate before し can also precede a polite main ending, as in the examples above.

### One し, and an unstated conclusion

> 安いし、ここにしましょう。  
> （やすいし、ここにしましょう。）  
> It is inexpensive, for one thing; let's choose this place.

Only one reason is stated. し presents it as a consideration, often leaving room for more.
It **does not logically guarantee that a second reason exists** or that the speaker has counted the reasons.

In conversation, even the conclusion can be left understood:

> 今日はもう遅いし……。  
> （きょうはもうおそいし……。）  
> It is already late today, and ...

In an appropriate conversation, the listener might infer "let's stop" or "let's go home."
The sentence fragment does not identify that conclusion on its own.
Sentence-final し can also sound insistent or defensive; it is not an automatic politeness device.

### し versus から and ので

**から** and **ので** explicitly connect a reason with a consequence. **し** can accumulate considerations
or serve as a reason without spelling out an exhaustive explanation.

Both **安いし、ここにしよう**, "it is inexpensive, for one thing; let's choose this place,"
and **安いから、ここにしよう**, "let's choose this place because it is inexpensive,"
can fit a decision about a restaurant.
**から does not mean that the reason is the only one**, just as し does not promise a second reason.
The difference concerns how the explanation is presented, not a rigid one-reason/two-reason count.

## て, くて, and で: connecting is not always listing examples

Compare two descriptions of a day:

| Sentence | Framing |
| --- | --- |
| 昨日は掃除をして、洗濯をしました。 | Yesterday I cleaned and did the laundry: a straightforward connection between actions. |
| 昨日は掃除をしたり、洗濯をしたりしました。 | Yesterday I did things like cleaning and laundry: representative activities. |

**掃除（そうじ）** means cleaning; **洗濯（せんたく）** means laundry.
The first can readily be understood as a sequence in a narrative.
But **て does not always mean "and then"**, and it does not declare an exhaustive list.

Adjectives and noun predicates also connect:

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| い-adjective: い becomes くて | この店は安くて便利です。 | This shop is inexpensive and convenient. |
| な-adjective: use で | この部屋は静かで明るいです。 | This room is quiet and bright. |
| Noun predicate: use で | 姉は医者で、兄は教師です。 | My older sister is a doctor, and my older brother is a teacher. |

**便利（べんり）** means convenient; **明るい（あかるい）** means bright;
**医者（いしゃ）** means doctor; **教師（きょうし）** means teacher.
The adjective **いい**, "good," uses **よくて**, not **いくて**.
These are basic affirmative connections, not the full positive/negative conjugation system.
For those foundations, use the [verb-form tables](japanese-verb-foundations.md) and
[adjective/noun-predicate tables](japanese-adjective-foundations.md).
The introductory connection exercises add ordinary verb-て and noun-predicate-で examples without replacing the
existing たり, し, or adjective-connection practice.

Quietness and brightness are simultaneous properties here, not successive events.
Connections with て can also express reasons and other relationships. Treating て as the "closed-list version of たり"
would therefore be misleading.

Whole sentences can instead be linked with **そして**, "and/then," or **それに**, "besides/on top of that":
these are not particles inserted between two nouns.

## Three close neighbors: も, か, and example-giving でも

### も: include each item

> コーヒーも紅茶もあります。  
> （コーヒーもこうちゃもあります。）  
> We have both coffee and black tea.

The **AもBも** pattern emphasizes that each item qualifies. It does not say that these are the only drinks.
With a negative predicate, the meaning changes accordingly:
**コーヒーも紅茶も飲みません**, "I drink neither coffee nor black tea."

The required case relation still matters:
**東京にも大阪にも行きました**, "I went to both Tokyo and Osaka."
Here **にも** preserves the destination particle に; do not mechanically replace every particle with も.

A single も also adds something to an understood comparison:
**紅茶もあります**, "we have black tea too."
That is addition, not an example marker with the same meaning as など.

### か: alternatives rather than joint inclusion

In a situation where you are choosing one drink:

> コーヒーか紅茶を選んでください。  
> （コーヒーかこうちゃをえらんでください。）  
> Please choose coffee or black tea.

This presents alternatives. It does not assert that both are chosen.
The one-drink situation supplies the requirement to choose one; do not turn every か into a universal
logical rule excluding all combinations in every context.
Question-final か is another use, not the function being illustrated here.

### でも: one possible suggestion

> 少し休みましょう。ジュースでも飲みませんか。  
> （すこしやすみましょう。ジュースでものみませんか。）  
> Let's take a short break. Shall we have some juice or something?

Juice is offered as one possible choice. A second drink does not have to be named.
This is different from the "even" sense in **子供でもできる**, "even a child can do it,"
and from the concessive **ても／でも** discussed alongside [のに](noni-and-concessive-contrasts.md).
The surrounding sentence selects the interpretation; the spelling でも alone is not enough.

## Connections to noun-based grammar

The [formal-nouns article](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md) already develops three useful neighbors:

| Construction | Connection to this topic |
| --- | --- |
| Nのような + noun | Can introduce category examples: ピアノのような楽器, "instruments such as the piano," in an example-giving context. It can also express resemblance. |
| Nのほかに | Adds something outside what was already named: パンのほかに, "besides bread." |
| うえに | Adds a further fact: 安いうえに便利だ, "it is inexpensive and, on top of that, convenient." |

**楽器（がっき）** means musical instrument.
**ピアノなどの楽器** can likewise mean "instruments such as the piano."
But resemblance and giving an actual category member as an example are not always the same:
ような has both uses, and など is not a universal replacement.

These are related ways of organizing information, not isolated competitors that must each win a different sentence.
For the fuller attachment patterns and qualifications, follow the linked article.

An unspecified quantity such as **何冊かの本（なんさつかのほん）**, some books, is not the same job as
listing representative members. See [quantity, ranges and frequency](quantity-ranges-and-frequency.md)
for counter quantities, distribution and short set-membership bridges; the listing exercises keep their owners.

## Further forms to recognize, not master all at once

The following is a recognition map, not a complete formation or substitution chart:

| Forms | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| やら…やら, だの…だの | Further listing patterns whose expressive tone and context deserve their own treatment. |
| なり…なり | Representative alternatives, often possible choices or courses of action. |
| NにN | An additive noun-listing use of に, distinct from its destination and location uses. |
| および, ならびに | Written/formal ways of coordinating items. |
| または, もしくは | Written/formal alternatives: "or." |
| だけでなく…も, Nに加えて | "Not only ... but also ..." and "in addition to N." 加えて is read くわえて. |
| AにしろBにしろ, AにせよBにせよ | "Whether A or B": a bridge to alternative conditions, not a statement that both occurred. |

These forms are not all interchangeable, and formal or legal writing can distinguish words that share a broad English gloss.
Likewise, なんか／なんて, uncertainty/reporting とか, and specialized sentence-final uses need more than an "etc." translation.

## Read the complete message

An open list, a single example, two included items, and two alternatives are different ideas.
Before choosing a connector, ask what is being named, which facts are asserted, and whether the speaker is reporting,
suggesting, giving reasons, or contrasting possibilities.

More than one formulation can fit the same situation. **や versus とか**, or **し versus から** in a reason-giving context,
may involve framing and register rather than one grammatical sentence and one error.
Learn those differences without inventing a unique answer where ordinary usage permits several.
