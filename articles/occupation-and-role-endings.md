# Japanese occupation and role endings: 者, 家, 師, 士, 手, 員, and 屋

**Japanese endings such as 者, 家, and 師 help describe what someone does, what they specialize in, or what position they hold. They are not a ranking of how honorable a profession is.** There are useful patterns, but established words and their contexts still decide the meaning.

Compare **[研究者](../words/研究者.md)（けんきゅうしゃ）**, "researcher," with **[研究員](../words/研究員.md)（けんきゅういん）**, "research staff member." One person can be both: the first identifies their activity, while the second presents them through an organizational role.

This article explains seven common endings, how they attach, and what they contribute to existing and newly formed words. It is a practical guide to interpretation, not an exhaustive list of occupation names or a history of each kanji.

## 1. What should we call these endings?

**Person-denoting suffixes** is a useful umbrella: **人を表す接尾辞（ひとをあらわすせつびじ）**. The term **接尾語（せつびご）** is also used for "suffix."

**Occupational suffixes** describes only part of their use. A 参加者, "participant," need not be doing a job, and an 努力家, "diligent person," is being described by a characteristic rather than a profession.

The boundary between a suffix and the second element of a compound varies by analysis. Dictionaries may label an item a suffix, a word-forming element, or a noun used like a suffix. Here, **ending** is a convenient descriptive label, not a claim that all seven have identical grammar.

These are also not simply **honorific suffixes** like さん. Naming someone's occupation and addressing someone respectfully are different functions.

### How the parts attach

Learn both the ending and the kind of expression it follows:

| Formation | Result | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 研究 + 者 | 研究者（けんきゅうしゃ） | Researcher |
| 漫画 + 家 | 漫画家（まんがか） | Manga artist |
| 研究 + 員 | 研究員（けんきゅういん） | Research staff member |
| 読み + 手 | 読み手（よみて） | Reader |
| パン + 屋 | パン屋（パンや） | Bakery; baker or person running it, depending on context |

In **研究者**, the base is the noun **研究**, "research," rather than the full verb **研究する**, "do research."

In **読み手**, the base is the verb's **ます-stem**: the part before ます in **読みます**, namely **読み**. It is not the dictionary form 読む. This is a particular word-formation pattern, not a rule that every ending attaches to every verb's ます-stem.

Readings also belong to the formation. **手** is **て** in 読み手 but **しゅ** in **歌手（かしゅ）**, "singer." **者** is **しゃ** in 研究者 but **じゃ** in **患者（かんじゃ）**, "patient." Do not choose freely between those readings.

## 2. The main tendencies

The table gives useful starting points, not definitions that predict every word:

| Ending | Typical contribution in words for people | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **[者](../kanji/者.md)（しゃ／じゃ）** | A person identified by an activity, involvement, or condition | 研究者 - researcher; 参加者（さんかしゃ） - participant |
| **[家](../kanji/家.md)（か）** | A practitioner or specialist in a field; also someone characterized by a disposition | 漫画家 - manga artist; 専門家（せんもんか） - expert |
| **[師](../kanji/師.md)（し）** | A practitioner of a specialized skill or art; also teaching-related roles | 医師（いし） - physician; 美容師（びようし） - hairdresser; [教師](../words/教師.md)（きょうし） - teacher |
| **[士](../kanji/士.md)（し）** | An established skilled or qualified role, including many professional titles | 弁護士（べんごし） - lawyer; 建築士（けんちくし） - architect |
| **[手](../kanji/手.md)（しゅ／て）** | A performer, operator, or person carrying out an activity | 歌手 - singer; [運転手](../words/運転手.md)（うんてんしゅ） - driver; 読み手 - reader |
| **[員](../kanji/員.md)（いん）** | Membership, staffing, or an assigned role in an organization | 会社員（かいしゃいん） - company employee; 研究員 - research staff member |
| **[屋](../kanji/屋.md)（や）** | A shop, seller, or trade practitioner; also a characterization of someone | 本屋（ほんや） - bookshop/bookseller; パン屋 - bakery/baker |

Some tempting shortcuts fail:

- **者 does not always mean the person doing an action.** **被害者（ひがいしゃ）**, "victim," identifies someone affected by an event.
- **家 does not always mean an expert or professional.** **努力家（どりょくか）**, "diligent person," and **愛好家（あいこうか）**, "enthusiast," need not name occupations.
- **手 does not require manual labor.** 歌手 identifies a singer, not someone working with their hands.
- **員 does not necessarily mean a paid employee.** **会員（かいいん）**, "member," can refer to membership of a club or association.
- **屋 does not always identify a person.** 本屋 often refers to the shop itself.

These observations concern the illustrated uses. They do not exhaust the meanings or readings of the individual kanji.

For example, 士 also occurs in the historical word **[武士](../words/武士.md)（ぶし）**, "samurai; warrior,"
not just in modern professional titles.

## 3. Contrasts are more useful than a prestige scale

### 研究者 and 研究員: activity versus organizational role

> 彼女は言語の研究者です。  
> She is a researcher who studies language.

> 彼女は大学の研究員です。  
> She is a research staff member at a university.

The first tells us about her research activity or identity; the second tells us about a position. Both could describe the same person.

**員 does not make someone a less accomplished researcher**, and 者 does not establish a particular employment status. An institution may give 研究員 a specific place in its job structure, but that comes from the institution, not from a universal rank encoded by 員.

### 作者 and 作家: the creator of a work versus a creative practitioner

These share the character 作, but they are established words with different uses:

| Word | Useful distinction |
| --- | --- |
| **[作者](../words/作者.md)（さくしゃ）** | The person who created a work: its author or maker |
| **[作家](../words/作家.md)（さっか）** | A creative practitioner; often a writer, especially a novelist, but also used for creators in other arts |

> この小説の作者は誰ですか。  
> Who is the author of this novel?

> 彼女は作家です。  
> She is a writer.

The first identifies someone's relationship to a particular work. The second describes a creative activity or identity. Being the 作者 of something does not by itself establish a career, and being called a 作家 does not certify fame, income, or a qualification.

Notice the reading **さっか**, not さくか. Meaningful components do not eliminate the need to learn the whole word's pronunciation.

This contrast illustrates why 家 often fits creative practitioners. It does **not** establish a universal rule that 者 is an amateur and 家 a professional.

### 師 and 士: skill and qualifications overlap

**師** often suggests a specialized practice, while **士** occurs in many skilled or qualified titles. But neither "師 = practical work, 士 = intellectual work" nor "only 士 requires a license" works.

For example, **美容師** and **弁護士** are both regulated professions in Japan, despite their different endings. The requirements belong to each profession and its governing rules, not to the choice of character alone.

Likewise, **医師** and **[医者](../words/医者.md)（いしゃ）** can refer to the same physician. They are not different qualification levels obtained by changing the ending.

There is also a decisive counterexample to "師 means an honorable specialist":

> 詐欺師（さぎし）  
> A swindler or con artist.

The word concerns deception, not moral respectability. The skill-related association does not guarantee approval.

Dictionaries separately record **師 as a respectful title after certain religious figures' names**. That honorific use is not the same function as the occupation-forming ending in 美容師. Keep the functions separate instead of importing respect into every word containing 師.

### 家 and 屋: evaluation can change, but context matters

Compare:

| Word | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| **[政治家](../words/政治家.md)（せいじか）** | Politician: the ordinary occupational label |
| **政治屋（せいじや）** | A derogatory label for a politician characterized as pursuing personal advantage |

Here, replacing the ending changes the evaluation, not merely the job description.

But it does not follow that **屋 is inherently insulting**. 本屋 and パン屋 are ordinary shop or trade names. In other professional descriptions, 屋 can convey familiarity, pride in practical work, self-deprecation, or contempt. The dictionary explicitly recognizes these context-dependent attitudes.

Nor is 家 reserved for morally admirable people: 政治家 names a role without guaranteeing that its holder is a good politician.

The practical lesson is to learn the **whole contrast and its setting**. Do not replace an established occupation name with a 屋 formation and assume that you have produced an equally neutral synonym.

## 4. How the patterns help with new names

### 漫画家 fits an existing word family

**漫画家（まんがか）** is now an established word, not a recent coinage. Nevertheless, it provides a useful illustration of how a name fits an existing pattern:

| Field | Person |
| --- | --- |
| 漫画（まんが） - manga | 漫画家 - manga artist |
| 写真（しゃしん） - photography | 写真家（しゃしんか） - photographer |
| 小説（しょうせつ） - fiction/novels | 小説家（しょうせつか） - novelist |

In this family, 家 presents someone as a creative practitioner in a field. That makes 漫画家 understandable alongside the other words.

This is a **present-day structural explanation**, not a documented account of the word's invention. It does not show who first coined 漫画家, whether they consciously modeled it on another word, or whether they intended to elevate manga's status.

The character 家 also means "house" in other uses, but that does not make a manga artist literally a household or imply that the occupation is hereditary. Use the relevant person-denoting sense rather than inventing an etymology from a kanji mnemonic.

### Contemporary roles can use different naming strategies

Consider two established digital-era labels:

| Expression | Pattern and meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **配信者（はいしんしゃ）** | 配信, "distribution/transmission," + 者; a content distributor, including an online streamer |
| **ブロガー** | A borrowed word, "blogger," rather than a name built with one of these kanji endings |

配信者 fits the pattern of identifying a person by an activity. That description need not tell us whether streaming is their paid occupation. The same caution applies to ブロガー.

The examples show that new activities need not all receive the same type of name. Existing Japanese word-formation patterns and borrowed vocabulary can coexist.

### Analogy helps, but does not guarantee acceptance

**Analogy** means modeling a new expression on familiar ones. The tendencies above make some choices easier to interpret: 者 can identify involvement in an activity, 員 can identify a staff role, and 家 can suggest a field-based practice.

**Productivity** is how readily a pattern can extend to new words. It is not the same as having many familiar examples. Dictionaries distinguish endings that combine relatively freely from ones restricted to particular formations; none of the tables here is permission to attach every ending to every base.

When interpreting or proposing a new name:

1. **Check the existing term first.** The established name may use a different ending, a borrowed word, or a longer expression.
2. **Identify what the name should emphasize.** Activity, creative practice, organizational role, and trade are different perspectives; prestige is not the main selection rule.
3. **Check the whole formation.** The base, reading, meaning, and register must work together. A recognizable meaning does not make a word idiomatic or an official professional title.

For an unfamiliar role, a descriptive phrase may be clearer than a new compact label. For example, **新しい技術を研究する人**, "a person who researches a new technology," describes an activity without inventing a title or implying a credential.

Suffix patterns are therefore especially useful for **recognizing and remembering** words. For a word you want to use yourself, check established usage; if you are deliberately coining a label, make its intended meaning clear.

## 5. Check the distinction

1. Is a 研究員 necessarily less expert than a 研究者?
2. Does 師 guarantee that the person is respected or that the occupation is licensed?
3. Does 政治屋 show that パン屋 is also derogatory?
4. Does the structure of 漫画家 prove why the word was historically coined?

**Answers:** 1. No; the labels foreground different aspects, and one person can be both. 2. No; individual words and professional rules decide those matters. 3. No; evaluation belongs to the particular expression and context. 4. No; understanding a present-day pattern is not evidence of its historical motivation.

**Learn the ending as a clue, the whole word as vocabulary, and the context as the guide to attitude.**
