# Wanting to act, wanting a thing and wanting an outcome

**読みたい, 本がほしい and 読んでほしい all involve desire, but they do not assign the same roles.**
Ask whose wish is being described, who would perform the action, and what is wanted.
Do not replace those questions with the shortcut "たい means I; てほしい means you."

The [N5 wants lesson](wants-plans-and-invitations.md) introduces たい, intentions and invitations.
This follow-up compares it with **がほしい** and **てほしい**, which the chosen primary curriculum
places at N4. A few quotation/question interpretation tasks use only already introduced N5 grammar.
The complete required task, not every word on this page, determines an exercise's study level.

## Three wishes about the same book

| Sentence | Reading | What is wanted? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 私はこの本がほしい。 | わたしはこのほんがほしい。 | I want this book: the thing itself. |
| 私はこの本を読みたい。 | わたしはこのほんをよみたい。 | I want to read this book: I would do the reading. |
| 私は妹にこの本を読んでほしい。 | わたしはいもうとにこのほんをよんでほしい。 | I want my younger sister to read this book. |

Reading a borrowed book does not require wanting to own it. Wanting a book does not by itself say
whether you intend to read it, lend it or keep it as a gift. The first sentence does not encode
a purchase or a particular way of obtaining the book.

In the third sentence, **私** is the desire-holder and **妹** is the intended reader. The に phrase
identifies the desired actor in this construction; it is not the desired book. The sister need not
be the listener: you could be telling a friend about your wish.

Compare the predicate, not just a particle. **この本が読みたい** can also express wanting to read
this book. With たい, both を and が can mark an ordinary direct object in suitable contexts;
there is no universal "stronger desire" scale that makes one wrong. This does not make every に,
を or が interchangeable. The [basic-particle article](japanese-nouns-and-basic-particles.md)
and [topic/contrast article](wa-ga-mo-topic-and-inclusion.md) explain why roles and context matter.

## Build the complete construction

| Desired meaning | Attachment | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Want to perform an action | Verb ます-stem + たい | 読みます → 読みたい |
| Want a thing | Noun + が + ほしい | 辞書がほしい |
| Want an action or state to happen | Verb て-form + ほしい | 読んで + ほしい |
| Want an action not to happen | Ordinary verb ない-form + で + ほしい | 読まないでほしい |

辞書（じしょ） means "dictionary." The noun pattern also permits abstract wants such as
時間がほしい（じかんがほしい）, "I want time"; "thing" is not a restriction to physical objects.
Topic/contrast wording such as その辞書はほしい is possible too. The table introduces the
ordinary が frame, not a ban on every other discourse frame.

**たい** and **ほしい** have ordinary i-adjective-like endings:

| Time/polarity | Want to read | Want a dictionary | Want someone to read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Affirmative nonpast | 読みたい | 辞書がほしい | 読んでほしい |
| Negative nonpast | 読みたくない | 辞書がほしくない | 読んでほしくない |
| Affirmative past | 読みたかった | 辞書がほしかった | 読んでほしかった |
| Negative past | 読みたくなかった | 辞書がほしくなかった | 読んでほしくなかった |

For example, 読みたかった is よみたかった; 読んでほしくなかった is よんでほしくなかった.
The desire ending carries this tense: **読んでほしかった** says that the wish existed before,
not that the reading necessarily happened. **読んだほしい** is not the way to build that past wish.
The past wish may or may not continue now; the past form alone does not settle that.

These plain forms can take です in ordinary polite speech: 読みたいです, ほしかったです.
Negative polite alternatives include ほしくないです and ほしくありません. A formation task
requesting the plain endings in the chart is not declaring those polite alternatives ungrammatical.
The [adjective foundations](japanese-adjective-foundations.md) explain the wider paradigm.

ほしい is commonly written in kana, especially after a verb; **欲しい** is the corresponding
dictionary spelling and is also accepted in these exercises. Its inflections include 欲しかった,
欲しくない and 欲しくなかった. Do not attach たい directly to a noun: 本がたい is not
"I want a book."

## Questions and reports change the perspective

An ordinary statement often expresses the speaker's own desire, while a question can ask about
the listener's. A direct quotation has its own speaker. None of these makes たい grammatically
restricted to the person narrating the whole conversation.

> 姉は妹に「この本が読みたいです」と言いました。  
> あねはいもうとに「このほんがよみたいです」といいました。  
> My older sister told my younger sister, "I want to read this book."

The older sister is both the quoted desire-holder and the intended reader. The younger sister
is the person addressed, not therefore the reader. The narrator is neither participant in that wish.

> 姉は妹に「私に写真を撮ってほしいですか」と聞きました。  
> あねはいもうとに「わたしにしゃしんをとってほしいですか」とききました。  
> My older sister asked my younger sister, "Do you want me to take a photograph?"

Here the question asks about the **younger sister's** wish, and the proposed photographer is
the **older sister**: quoted 私 refers to the person speaking inside the quotation.
The question does not assert that the younger sister wants this, or that anyone takes the photograph.

Compare **写真を撮りたいですか**（しゃしんをとりたいですか）, "Do you want to take a photograph?"
That asks whether the person addressed wants to be the photographer. Changing たい to てほしい
can change who would act, not merely make the same sentence more polite.

Japanese often omits participants when context identifies them:

> この本、読んでほしいです。  
> このほん、よんでほしいです。  
> I want [someone] to read this book.

In a conversation about your sister's reading, that someone may be your sister, not the listener.
Without that context, do not invent a unique actor from the ending alone. The exercises state
the relevant participants; they do not grade guesses about an unavailable conversation.
See [quotations and contextual endings](explanations-quotations-and-endings.md) for the reporting frame.

## Two places for negation

> この箱を開けないでほしい。  
> このはこをあけないでほしい。  
> I want [you/someone] not to open this box.

> この箱を開けてほしくない。  
> このはこをあけてほしくない。  
> I don't want [you/someone] to open this box.

The first uses **開けない + で + ほしい**: the desired action is negative.
The second uses **開けて + ほしくない**: the desire expression has a negative ending.
In this context, both can convey opposition to opening the box. An English "don't want" gloss
does not make one uniquely correct, and different attachment positions do not guarantee
different practical wishes in every conversation.

This is not a promise that the two are interchangeable in every register, discourse or sentence.
Nor is ないでほしい necessarily a request to a person: a wish that it not rain also uses it.
For practice, some tasks explicitly request the negative action plus affirmative ほしい;
others request the unchanged て-form plus negative ほしい. The other formulation may express
a similar wish but does not perform the requested conversion.

The same scope distinction can be carried into the past:
**開けないでほしかった** means "I wanted [someone] not to open it";
**開けてほしくなかった** means "I didn't want [someone] to open it."
Neither proves what happened. **開けたくない**, by contrast, is the desire-holder's own
unwillingness to open it, not simply another spelling of wanting someone else not to.

## An outcome need not have a willing actor

> 庭の桜が咲いてほしい。  
> にわのさくらがさいてほしい。  
> I want the cherry tree in the garden to bloom.

> 週末は雨が降らないでほしい。  
> しゅうまつはあめがふらないでほしい。  
> I hope it does not rain at the weekend.

These are desired outcomes, not orders to a tree or to the rain. **桜が咲く** is the event
whose occurrence is wanted; **雨が降らない** is the situation being wished for.
The が after 桜 or 雨 belongs inside that event description. It does not make the tree or
rain an object the speaker wants to acquire, as in 辞書がほしい.

Desired states work too: **無事でいてほしい**（ぶじでいてほしい） means "I want [someone]
to remain safe." Daijisen includes this example under 欲しい, sense 3. It is not necessary
to recast every use as someone deliberately obeying a command.

The sentence can function as a request in an appropriate conversation, but **ほしいです
is not an automatic politeness formula**. The wish, the social appropriateness of saying it,
the addressee's agreement and the actual outcome are separate matters.

## Optional bridges: observation and receiving an action

### What someone shows versus what they said

**読みたがる**（よみたがる） describes showing a desire to read; **ほしがる／欲しがる**
describes showing a desire for something. Form たがる from the たい form by replacing its
final い with がる: 読みたい → 読みたがる. This is not the verb's past た-form.
Likewise, ほしい → ほしがる. These patterns conjugate as verbs:
読みたがっている, ほしがっていた.

> 妹はその本をほしがっています。  
> いもうとはそのほんをほしがっています。  
> My younger sister is showing that she wants that book.

This presents outwardly shown desire. A direct quotation such as
**妹は「その本がほしい」と言いました** reports what she said.
Do not mechanically replace every third-person たい／ほしい with がる, or claim to
have direct access to someone's feelings merely because they are named in a sentence.
The observation forms are a recognition bridge here, not additional scored targets.

### Wanting to receive someone's action

> 友達に手伝ってもらいたいです。  
> ともだちにてつだってもらいたいです。  
> I want to have my friend help me.

This combines **手伝ってもらう**, receiving the action of helping, with **たい**:
もらいます → もらいたい. The desire-holder wants to receive the help; the friend performs it.
Compare **友達に手伝ってほしいです**, which directly presents the friend's helping
as the desired action. In this scenario the wishes can overlap, but the receiving perspective
is not meaningless, and neither wording is automatically the more tactful request.

The [giving/receiving article](giving-receiving-and-viewpoint.md) introduces the basic receiver
perspective. The full てもらう construction is a separate N4 bridge; it is not just a noun
gift with another verb inserted. Do not mechanically substitute てもらいたい into every
weather wish or assume that every desired outcome is a received favor.
Benefactive auxiliaries, causatives and honorific request systems are not assessed in this batch.
The [benefactive-actions companion](benefactive-actions.md) now develops ordinary action-based giving/receiving
and favor questions. It preserves the wish/action distinction rather than duplicating these desire exercises.

## What the practice asks you to do

- Choose a construction for the stated wish without changing its actor or replacing a thing with an action.
- Interpret who holds the proposed/reported wish and who would act; a question does not assert its own answer.
- Form the named construction with the requested tense, polarity and negation position.
- Distinguish a desired thing from the occurrence or nonoccurrence of a stated event.

Role answers use **前者（ぜんしゃ）**, the first-mentioned sister, and
**後者（こうしゃ）**, the second-mentioned sister. Other interpretation answers use
**物（もの）**, a wanted thing itself, and **出来事（できごと）**, the stated event.
Every interpretation question defines its labels in a fixed order before answering.
They are not references to the shuffled button order or vocabulary-level prerequisites.

Paired forms and roles deliberately reuse some scenes. A successful conversion is not proof of
unrestricted conversational mastery, and examples do not imply that a desire was fulfilled.
For desire versus intention, return to [wants and plans](wants-plans-and-invitations.md#2-wanting-is-not-the-same-as-intending-たい-and-つもり).
For obligations and advice, see [noun-based grammar](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md).
For what someone can do rather than wants to do, see [ability and perception](ability-and-perception.md).
Those are useful neighboring questions, not reasons to add every normative expression here.
