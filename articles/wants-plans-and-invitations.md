# Wants, plans, invitations and offers

"I want to go", "I intend to go", "Let's go" and "Shall I go?" can concern the same trip,
but they do different things. Start with **whose action is involved** and **what the speaker is doing**:
expressing a wish, reporting an intention, proposing a shared activity, or offering help.

This is an N5 entry guide. It builds on [verb forms](japanese-verb-foundations.md) and
[adjective forms](japanese-adjective-foundations.md); the short higher-level desire comparison near the end
is optional, not a prerequisite for its exercises.

## 1. Want to do something: stem + たい

Attach たい to the verb's **ます-stem**, the part before ます:

| Dictionary verb | ます form | Stem + たい |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 読む (よむ, read) | 読みます | 読みたい |
| 飲む (のむ, drink) | 飲みます | 飲みたい |
| 食べる (たべる, eat) | 食べます | 食べたい |
| する (do) | します | したい |
| 来る (くる, come) | 来ます (きます) | 来たい (きたい) |

If starting from a dictionary verb, use the actual stem operation: a godan verb changes its final
u-row sound to the corresponding i-row sound (読む → 読み); an ichidan verb drops る (食べる → 食べ).
する and 来る have し and き. Not every verb ending in る is ichidan: see the
[verb guide](japanese-verb-foundations.md) for that distinction.

> 私は京都に行きたいです。  
> わたしは きょうとに いきたいです。  
> I want to go to Kyoto.

The たい part changes like an い-adjective:

| Desired meaning | Plain form | With ordinary polite です |
| --- | --- | --- |
| want to read | 読みたい | 読みたいです |
| do not want to read | 読みたくない | 読みたくないです |
| wanted to read | 読みたかった | 読みたかったです |
| did not want to read | 読みたくなかった | 読みたくなかったです |

Do not make 読むたい or 読みたいでした. In the last two rows, the desire is located in the past.
Neither form tells you by itself whether the reading happened.
Polite negative 読みたくありません and past-negative 読みたくありませんでした are also possible;
the linked inflection drills explicitly request the plain たい form before an already printed です.

### Keep the other participants' roles

For an ordinary desired action, the thing acted on can often take **を or が**:

> 水を飲みたいです。 / 水が飲みたいです。  
> みずを のみたいです。 / みずが のみたいです。  
> I want to drink water.

Both work in this ordinary context. Do not use a universal "が means stronger desire" scale to reject を.
Their distribution and emphasis depend on the whole construction and context.
This is not permission to replace every particle with が:

> 京都に行きたいです。 / 京都へ行きたいです。  
> きょうとに いきたいです。 / きょうとへ いきたいです。  
> I want to go to Kyoto.

京都 is the destination, not the thing consumed or an ordinary object of 行く.
京都まで行きたいです can instead frame Kyoto as the intended travel limit, "I want to go as far as Kyoto".
See [noun phrases and particles](japanese-nouns-and-basic-particles.md) for roles, direction and limits.

### Whose wish?

A plain statement commonly expresses the speaker's own wish. A question can ask the listener's:
何を飲みたいですか (なにを のみたいですか), "What do you want to drink?"
Quoted or reported speech and narrative viewpoints allow other desire-holders.
Do not learn "たい is grammatically first person only".
When reporting someone else's private feelings, make the basis and viewpoint appropriate instead of simply
assuming access to their mind. The more advanced reporting patterns are outside this introduction.

## 2. Wanting is not the same as intending: たい and つもり

| What you are saying | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a wish | 京都に行きたいです。 | I want to go to Kyoto. |
| an intention | 京都に行くつもりです。 | I intend to go to Kyoto. |

たい uses the stem; the ordinary future-intention pattern uses a **plain nonpast verb + つもりです**.
An intention can concern next week or a much more distant goal. It is not a guarantee of success.

> 京都に行きたいです。でも、今年は行くつもりはありません。  
> きょうとに いきたいです。でも、ことしは いく つもりは ありません。  
> I want to go to Kyoto. But I have no intention of going this year.

> 行きたくないですが、仕事で行くつもりです。  
> いきたくないですが、しごとで いく つもりです。  
> I do not want to go, but I intend to go for work.

A person can also both want and intend the same action. A context saying only "I am going next week"
does not necessarily separate the two. The new contrast tasks explicitly establish which attitude
is being expressed; the existing intention exercises are reused rather than copied.

For a chosen negative plan, 行かないつもりです is useful. 行くつもりはありません denies an intention to go.
They can overlap in context without being identical in every situation.
For negative and past intentions, and the separate harder "thought I had done it" use, see
[the existing つもり discussion](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md#つもり-a-plan-or-a-belief-about-what-has-been-done).
The completion-belief contrast is optional and keeps its existing study level.

For the N4 continuation, see [volition, plans and goals](volition-plans-and-goals.md):
plain 行こう, intention with 行こうと思う, and planned activities with 予定.
It reuses these intention and polite-invitation foundations rather than creating new copies.

## 3. Propose, invite or offer: ましょう, ませんか, ましょうか

All three use the ます-stem:

| Form | Useful ordinary function | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| stem + ましょう | propose doing something; "let's ..." | 一緒に読みましょう。 |
| stem + ませんか | invite someone; "would you like to ...?" | 一緒に読みませんか。 |
| stem + ましょうか | ask about a proposed action; "shall we / shall I ...?" | 一緒に読みましょうか。 |

一緒に (いっしょに) means "together"; the examples propose reading together.
For する, use しましょう／しませんか／しましょうか; for 来る, use
来ましょう／来ませんか／来ましょうか, all with き as the verb reading here.

These are **not three mutually exclusive social situations**.
ませんか often leaves an invitation open for the listener's response, while ましょう can present the proposal more directly.
ましょうか asks for agreement or a decision. Relationships, tone and context matter; "polite" does not establish a
universal ranking, and more than one wording can be natural for the same shared activity.

The negative-looking ません in an invitation does not mean that the proposed activity is "not reading".
In another context, a genuine negative question can ask whether someone does not do something.
Read the situation rather than classifying every ませんか as an invitation.

### ましょうか can offer the speaker's action

> 荷物を運びましょうか。  
> にもつを はこびましょうか。  
> Shall I carry the luggage?

> 手伝いましょうか。  
> てつだいましょうか。  
> Shall I help?

[運ぶ](../words/運ぶ.md) means carrying or transporting; [手伝う](../words/手伝う.md) means helping with a task.
In a helping situation, ましょうか can offer **my** action, not ask both of us to do it.
In 一緒に運びましょうか (いっしょに はこびましょうか), it can propose carrying something together.
Identify the actor from the context and wording; か alone does not tell you who acts.

The invitation practice accepts fitting ましょう／ませんか／ましょうか alternatives together.
Separate formation drills name the requested form, and the actor-interpretation tasks explain their response labels
before answering. None treats a named formation drill as unrestricted conversation mastery.

## 4. Choose an option, request an item, or ask "how about ...?"

### Noun + にする: choose that option

> 紅茶にします。  
> こうちゃに します。  
> I'll have tea / I'll choose tea.

にする presents a choice. The ordinary polite form is にします.
It need not concern buying something: 窓側の席にします
(まどがわの せきに します) means "I'll choose the window seat".
Compare [選ぶ](../words/選ぶ.md), "choose": making a choice does not by itself mean completing a purchase.
Other change/causative uses of する are separate from this selection pattern.

### Noun + をください: request that item

> 紅茶をください。  
> こうちゃを ください。  
> Tea, please.

The noun names what you want supplied, and を marks it. Compare verb **てください**, a request to perform an action,
in [requests and rules](japanese-requests-permission-and-rules.md).
Do not add する merely because the English translation uses "give".

At a cafe, 紅茶にします and 紅茶をください can both function naturally in ordering.
The first presents the selected option; the second directly requests the item.
The difference is not a rule that one must be wrong whenever the other fits.
The linked assembly drills state which construction to build rather than inventing a hidden menu-context distinction.

### Noun + はどうですか: context decides the question

> 土曜日はどうですか。  
> どようびは どうですか。  
> How about Saturday?

When arranging a meeting, this can propose Saturday as an option.
By contrast, 新しい学校はどうですか (あたらしい がっこうは どうですか),
"How is the new school?", asks for an impression or assessment.
The form is not always an invitation. These are supporting examples, not a new independently scored facet.
The [listing guide](japanese-listing-and-examples.md) has optional ways of suggesting examples and alternatives;
its harder patterns are not required distractors here.

## 5. Go or come to do something: stem + に + motion

> 図書館へ本を借りに行きます。  
> としょかんへ ほんを かりに いきます。  
> I will go to the library to borrow a book.

The purpose uses the **stem + に**: 借りに, not 借りるに.
Keep the other roles separate: 図書館へ is the destination, 本を is what will be borrowed.
For する, use しに, as in 勉強しに行きます (べんきょうしに いきます), "go to study".
For godan verbs, use the i-row stem: 買う → 買いに行く, "go to buy".

From the destination's viewpoint:

> 本を借りに来ました。  
> ほんを かりに きました。  
> I came to borrow a book.

来る locates motion toward the relevant reference location, often where the speaker is; 行く presents going to another place.
Do not choose 来る solely because the listener is at the destination: Japanese often uses 行く where English says
"come", as in 今行きます (いま いきます), "I'm coming".
Choose the viewpoint in context; do not replace every past 行った with 来た merely because the trip is over.
The new drills supply the go/come situation and request the purpose phrase, not every possible motion perspective.
Advanced ていく direction/aspect comparisons are optional in
[verb extensions](verb-extensions-direction-and-aspect.md), not required N5 choices here.

## Optional next step: wanting an action versus wanting it done

> 手伝いたいです。  
> てつだいたいです。  
> I want to help.

> 手伝ってほしいです。  
> てつだって ほしいです。  
> I want someone to help.

In the first sentence, the desire-holder wants to do the helping.
In the second, the desire-holder wants the helping to happen, ordinarily with someone else doing it.
The actor and desire-holder still depend on context: a question can ask what the listener wants.
てほしい can also concern a desired state, not only an action requested of a person.
Simply expressing a desire is not automatically a tactful request or a guarantee that someone will comply.

The [てほしい lesson](https://bunpro.jp/grammar_points/てほしい) and
[がほしい lesson](https://bunpro.jp/grammar_points/がほしい) are **N4 onward reading** in the chosen primary curriculum.
The latter concerns wanting a thing, rather than using たい with an action.
The [higher-level desire comparison](desire-actions-and-outcomes.md) explores these roles, negation and desired outcomes;
no N5 exercise here requires those forms,
たがる or the higher-level reporting patterns to choose an answer.
