# Explanations, quotations and sentence endings

A sentence can describe an event, connect it to a situation, report someone else's words, or invite a response.
Those are different jobs. A familiar English translation does not always choose one Japanese ending for us.

Compare:

| Japanese | A possible use |
| --- | --- |
| 今日は休みです。 | State that today is a day off. |
| 今日は休みなんです。 | Present that fact as relevant background or an explanation. |
| 「今日は休みです」と言いました。 | Report the quoted words. |
| 今日は休みですよ。 | Draw the listener's attention to the fact. |
| 今日は休みですね。 | Invite or acknowledge a shared understanding. |
| 明日は休みでしょう。 | Offer a conjecture about tomorrow, in an appropriate context. |

**今日（きょう）** means today, **明日（あした）** tomorrow, **休み（やすみ）** a day off or break,
and **言いました（いいました）** means said.
The table gives possible functions, not six mutually exclusive translations of an isolated English sentence.

## のだ and んです: connect a statement to its context

Someone wonders why the speaker has time to meet:

> 今日は休みなんです。  
> きょうはやすみなんです。  
> The thing is, today is my day off.

The day off is presented as relevant background. **のだ／のです** and the common contracted
**んだ／んです** can provide an explanation, background, a realization or an interpretation.
They do not simply make a statement more true, and they do not guarantee that an interpretation is correct.

The connection may already be understandable from the conversation; an explicit "because" clause is not required.
Conversely, adding んです to every sentence can impose an explanatory stance that the conversation does not need.

### Build from the predicate, not from its English translation

In an **affirmative nonpast noun or na-adjective** predicate, use **な** before explanatory の／ん.
Do not leave the ordinary bare だ there:

| Basic predicate | Plain explanatory form | Polite explanatory form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 学生だ | 学生なんだ／学生なのだ | 学生なんです／学生なのです |
| 静かだ | 静かなんだ／静かなのだ | 静かなんです／静かなのです |
| 来る | 来るんだ／来るのだ | 来るんです／来るのです |
| 安い | 安いんだ／安いのだ | 安いんです／安いのです |

**学生（がくせい）** is student, **静か（しずか）** quiet, **来る（くる）** come,
and **安い（やすい）** inexpensive.
Thus **学生なんです = 学生 + な + ん + です**. Here ん represents の; it is not **何（なん）**, "what."

The な rule is **not a rule to add な after every predicate involving a noun**:

| Predicate already showing time or negation | With explanatory んです |
| --- | --- |
| 学生だった | 学生だったんです |
| 静かだった | 静かだったんです |
| 学生ではない | 学生ではないんです |
| 安くない | 安くないんです |
| 来なかった | 来なかったんです |

**だった** is the ordinary plain past for these noun/na-adjective predicates.
**安くない（やすくない）** means not inexpensive; **来なかった（こなかった）** means did not come.
Keep that predicate's tense and polarity. For example, **静かだったなんです** wrongly adds the affirmative-nonpast な
after an already past predicate.

The exercises specify the final short **だ** or **です** ending separately from the time of the event being explained.
They also request short copulas inside the predicate, including past だった rather than expanded であった.
That is a bounded formation request, not a claim that every possible explanatory sentence must end in the nonpast.

### What contracts, and what does not follow from that

Spoken **んだ／んです** is ordinary standard Japanese, including polite んです.
Uncontracted **のだ／のです** also exists; it is not universally incorrect, obsolete or interchangeable in every register.
When a drill leaves contraction open, both fitting forms are accepted.

Do not turn this into an instruction to replace every の with ん.
In **本を読むのが好きです**, "I like reading books," の nominalizes the activity; it is not the same complete
explanatory construction. See the existing
[の／ん explanation](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md#の-ん-なの-and-なん) and
[nominalizing の](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md#a-particularly-important-neighbor-の).
The more specific explanatory conclusion **わけだ** is another optional
[later comparison](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md#the-explanatory-construction-わけだ), not a required answer here.

## の questions: ask with a contextual connection

After hearing about a friend's unusual weekday schedule, you ask:

> 今日は休みなの？  
> きょうはやすみなの？  
> Is today your day off?

The question connects to the situation. It need not express surprise, accusation or a demand for an explicit reason.
An ordinary short question without の may also be possible; の is not a compulsory casual question marker.

| Predicate | Contextual casual question |
| --- | --- |
| 学生だ | 学生なの？ |
| 暇だ | 暇なの？ |
| 来る | 来るの？ |
| 行かない | 行かないの？ |
| 学生だった | 学生だったの？ |

**暇（ひま）** means free or having time, and **行かない（いかない）** means does not/will not go.
The affirmative-nonpast nominal な appears again, but past **だった** does not acquire another な.
An ordinary polite counterpart is **学生なんですか？／学生なのですか？**.

The casual-question drills request the uncontracted final の and no added か or copula.
Do not transfer the の → ん contraction before だ／です mechanically to every standalone final の;
other conversational and regional forms need their own context.

## と and って: mark quoted content

> 友達は「明日は休みです」と言いました。  
> ともだちは「あしたはやすみです」といいました。  
> My friend said, "Tomorrow is a day off."

**友達（ともだち）** means friend. The と after the closing quotation marks identifies the quoted content.
It is not joining two nouns.

An exact direct quotation can preserve what was said: polite predicates, questions and even a quoted word.
You do **not** have to remove です inside quotation marks merely because と follows.

An ordinary plain nominal **predicate** is different from a bare quoted word:

| Quoted material | What it supplies |
| --- | --- |
| 「学生だ」 | A full ordinary plain affirmative predicate: "[someone] is a student." |
| 「学生です」 | A full ordinary polite affirmative predicate. |
| 「学生」 | The quoted word "student," which can itself be what someone said. |

Do not turn the first row into a universal ban on quoting a noun without だ.
The copula drills expressly request a full short plain or polite predicate, not a bare word, ellipsis or an expanded copula.

### Conversational って

**って** can replace the quotation marker in conversational reporting:

> 友達は「明日は休みです」って言いました。  
> ともだちは「あしたはやすみです」っていいました。  
> My friend said, "Tomorrow is a day off."

This can occur in friendly polite conversation. A supplied です or ました does not by itself make って impossible.
って is not limited to verbatim quotations: it can also report the content of what someone said.
The reporting verb may be understood in conversation, but our marker drills keep it printed.

Those drills explicitly request either the ordinary one-kana marker or the conversational doubled-consonant marker.
The other can be grammatical yet outside that requested form; the scene alone does not always distinguish them.
Topic-introducing って and larger reported-speech constructions are separate uses, not extra targets in this set.

### Which と is doing the work?

| Sentence | Role of と |
| --- | --- |
| 私と母が公園へ行きました。 | Joins the two named people. |
| 私は母と公園へ行きました。 | Marks the companion of the person going. |
| 母は「公園へ行きました」と言いました。 | Marks the content of the report. |

**私（わたし）** is I, **母（はは）** my mother, **公園（こうえん）** park,
and **行きました（いきました）** went.
The first two can describe the same trip through different grammatical frames.
The third reports words about a trip; quotation alone does not establish that the report is true.

Noun-list と is not an "only" particle, and companion と does not quantify how equally people participated.
See [noun lists and companions](japanese-listing-and-examples.md#と-join-the-named-nouns).
[Conditional と](japanese-conditionals.md) is yet another use; it is not included in these three response categories.

## よ and ね: consider the listener and the exchange

Suppose you know the shop is closed and a friend is about to enter:

> 今日は休みですよ。  
> きょうはやすみですよ。  
> It is closed today, you know.

Here よ draws attention to information relevant to the friend. It does not universally mean "new information";
reminding, correcting and other uses also depend on the exchange.

If you are checking a remembered schedule with someone:

> 明日は試験ですね。  
> あしたはしけんですね。  
> The exam is tomorrow, right?

**試験（しけん）** means examination. Here ね invites confirmation of the shared understanding.
In another exchange, ね can invite agreement or a shared reaction rather than a literal yes/no answer.
Neither particle has one universal English translation or a fixed rudeness score.

They can combine: **明日は試験ですよね** can present the understanding while seeking alignment.
That does not make every よ, ね and よね interchangeable in every context.

Keep ordinary predicate attachment:

| Full ordinary pattern | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Plain verb or i-adjective + ending | 行くよ／暑いね |
| Full plain nominal predicate + ending | 休みだよ／静かだね |
| Polite predicate + ending | 休みですよ／静かですね |

**暑い（あつい）** means hot. It does not take an extra plain だ in **暑いね**.
The full copula patterns shown here do not prohibit every conversational omission.
See [recoverable participants and context](wa-ga-mo-topic-and-inclusion.md#you-do-not-have-to-repeat-an-understood-subject-or-topic)
for another reason an isolated sentence can leave information unstated.

Our formation tasks name the requested ending function/form; interpretation tasks provide the conversational situation.
They do not score an imagined voice, insist that an English tag must always become ね, or prove that no other utterance would work.

## でしょう and だろう: conjecture is not a numerical probability

**でしょう** and **だろう** can present a conjecture; でしょう is the ordinary polite form and だろう its plain counterpart.
They can also seek confirmation in suitable contexts. A written ending alone does not recover every intended intonation.

| Base predicate | Polite conjectural form | Plain conjectural form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 雨が降る | 雨が降るでしょう | 雨が降るだろう |
| 電車が来ない | 電車が来ないでしょう | 電車が来ないだろう |
| 暑かった | 暑かったでしょう | 暑かっただろう |
| 休みだ | 休みでしょう | 休みだろう |
| 静かだった | 静かだったでしょう | 静かだっただろう |

**雨（あめ）** is rain, **降る（ふる）** fall as precipitation, and **電車（でんしゃ）** train.
**来ない（こない）** means does not/will not come; **暑かった（あつかった）** means was hot.
The ordinary nonpast nominal base does not retain a separate bare だ before でしょう／だろう.
The past **だった** does remain in **静かだったでしょう**.

Common contractions **でしょ／だろ** are accepted when the task leaves them open.
These drills request the short endings, not expanded copular conjectures.
Plain versus polite is a stated form request, not something inferred from whether the speakers are friends.
だろう is not reserved for men, and neither ending fixes a numerical confidence level.

An ordinary assertion such as **明日は休みです** is not automatically a proven fact about the world.
The interpretation drills distinguish how the message is presented in the stated scene:
conjecture versus an unqualified assertion, not "possibly false" versus "guaranteed true."

## Response labels and practice boundaries

Interpretation questions give these readings and operational meanings **before** answering, in fixed order:

| Labels | What they distinguish in these tasks |
| --- | --- |
| 列挙（れっきょ）／同行（どうこう）／引用（いんよう） | Joining named nouns, marking a companion, or marking quoted content. |
| 伝達（でんたつ）／確認（かくにん） | Conveying information versus seeking confirmation of a shared understanding in the specified exchange. |
| 推測（すいそく）／断定（だんてい） | Presenting a conjecture versus making an unqualified assertion in the specified scene. |

These are response categories with local definitions, not claims that every real utterance has only one communicative function.
Readings and meanings support the interface; memorizing their kanji is not the grammatical target.
Formation and matched interpretation are scaffolded practice, not a claim of unrestricted discourse mastery.

### Two short recognition notes, not new scored targets

**な／なあ** can add a reflective or admiring reaction: **すごいなあ**, "Wow, that is impressive."
Its use depends on context. Do not treat every final な as this use, or silently include every かな construction in it.

**お／ご** occur in conventional polite or honorific wording, such as **お茶（おちゃ）**, tea,
and **ご家族（ごかぞく）**, family. Learn the actual expression and its social use.
The native/Sino-Japanese preference is a tendency with exceptions, not permission to prefix any noun automatically.
This note does not teach the full honorific system or claim that every bare 茶 is rude.
