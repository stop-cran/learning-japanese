# Necessity, advice and instructions

"You need not do it," "you must not do it," "you must do it," and "I wish I had done it"
can contain similar-looking negative or conditional pieces. Read the **whole message**:
what action is being evaluated, when, and with what attitude?

This is the N4 continuation of [requests, permission and rules](japanese-requests-permission-and-rules.md).
That lesson still owns basic requests, permission, prohibition, なくては obligations and common contractions.
The [verb foundations](japanese-verb-foundations.md) and [conditional tables](japanese-conditionals.md)
provide the underlying forms.

## 1. Not required is different from prohibited

| Message | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not required | 今日は来なくてもいいです。 | You do not have to come today. |
| Prohibited | 今日は来てはいけません。 | You must not come today. |
| Required | 今日は来なければいけません。 | You have to come today. |

来る is read **くる**, but its negative is **来ない（こない）**.
In 来なくてもいい, it is acceptable **not to come**. That does not itself forbid coming,
predict absence or tell us whether the person wants to come. A no-need remark can also sound dismissive
in a particular conversation; the literal optionality pattern is not a guarantee of friendly intent.

To form it, replace the final **ない** of a negative verb with **なくても**, then add **いい／よい**:

| Dictionary form | Negative | No obligation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 行く（いく）, go | 行かない | 行かなくてもいい |
| 食べる（たべる）, eat | 食べない | 食べなくてもいい |
| する, do | しない | しなくてもいい |
| 来る（くる）, come | 来ない（こない） | 来なくてもいい（こなくてもいい） |

Polite examples end in **いいです／よいです**. The shorter **来なくていい** is also common;
omitting も is not a universal switch to friendliness or a promise of identical emphasis.
The formation drills retain も to practise the full pattern, not because the shorter version is wrong.

### A question is not already an answer

> 客「名前を書かなくてもいいですか。」係「はい、いいですよ。」  
> きゃく「なまえをかかなくてもいいですか。」かかり「はい、いいですよ。」  
> Visitor: "Is it all right not to write my name?" Staff: "Yes, that's fine."

The staff member accepts **omitting** the name. Compare a different policy:

> 客「名前を書いてもいいですか。」係「いいえ、書いてはいけません。」  
> きゃく「なまえをかいてもいいですか。」かかり「いいえ、かいてはいけません。」  
> Visitor: "May I write my name?" Staff: "No, you must not write it."

Track what the question asks and what the reply accepts or rejects. Do not classify every
question containing いい as permission to perform the action.
The interpretation tasks ask which policy is **expressed**, not every rule that could coexist with it.
General "even if" concession is a separate [clause-linking topic](japanese-conditionals.md).

## 2. Something is necessary; doing something is necessary

**必要（ひつよう）** is both a noun meaning "need/necessity" and a な-adjective meaning "necessary."
Its two common frames put the action or item in different places:

| Frame | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun + が必要だ | この切符が必要です。 | This ticket is necessary. |
| Nonpast verb + 必要がある | この説明を読む必要があります。 | It is necessary to read this explanation. |

Read **切符（きっぷ）**, **説明（せつめい）**, **読む（よむ）**.
In the first sentence, 切符 is what is needed; 必要 is the predicate.
In the second, 読む modifies the noun 必要: the need **to read** exists.
Do not insert the verb's ます ending directly before the noun: 読みます必要 is not this connection.

A nominalized action can also fit the first frame:
**説明を読むことが必要です**, "Reading the explanation is necessary."
This is an alternative attachment route, not a reason to insert こと into every direct verb + 必要 task.
Review [activities as nouns](noun-descriptions-and-activities.md) if that structure is unfamiliar.

Necessity is relative to the stated situation or goal, not automatically a law or moral duty.
**予約が必要でした**（よやくがひつようでした）, "A reservation was necessary," puts the necessity in the past;
it does not prove that somebody actually reserved.

### No need is not a need to refrain

- **読む必要はありません。** There is no need to read it.
- **読まない必要があります。** There is a need not to read it, in a context where refraining is necessary.

The second is not the ordinary way to say "you need not read."
Other no-need formulations include **読む必要がない／読む必要はない／読む必要はないです**,
with context-dependent emphasis and register.
They do not inherently prohibit reading or say that reading is impossible.

The existing [intention, no-need and no-alternative comparison](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md#mixed-contextual-selection-intention-no-need-or-no-other-option)
also uses ことはない and ほかない／しかない. Those harder mixed exercises remain separate;
learning this basic 必要 frame does not make every comparison involving it N4.

## 3. Obligations: evaluate not doing the action

| Whole construction | Example | Basic message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| なくては + いけない／ならない | 名前を書かなくてはいけない。 | I/you must write the name. |
| なければ + いけない／ならない | 名前を書かなければならない。 | I/you must write the name. |
| ないと + いけない | 名前を書かないといけない。 | I/you must write the name. |

Read **名前（なまえ）**, **書く（かく）**.
The unacceptable condition is **not writing**; these are not three prohibitions against writing.
The subject depends on the conversation.

For **なければ**, build the negative verb and replace its final ない with なければ:
書かない → 書かなければ; 食べない → 食べなければ.
For **ないと**, keep the negative verb and add と: 書かない → 書かないと.
Then supply the negative evaluation. Polite endings include **いけません／なりません**.

There is substantial overlap between these obligation constructions.
ならない often sounds more formal than いけない, but it is not forbidden in conversation.
There is no dependable ladder in which every ないと is stronger than every なければ,
or a personal-versus-social-duty test that uniquely chooses an ending.
Named formation tasks distinguish the connectors, not the only possible way to express a real-world duty.

### Contractions and omitted endings

| Full sequence | Contracted sequence |
| --- | --- |
| 書かなければいけません | 書かなきゃいけません |
| 食べなくてはなりません | 食べなくちゃなりません |
| 書かないといけない | 書かないと, when the evaluation is understood |

**なければ → なきゃ** and **なくては → なくちゃ** are different contractions.
A conversational contraction can retain a polite ending.
Expanding なきゃ as なくては may preserve a broad obligation meaning, but it is not the expansion
of that particular contracted sequence.

An understood obligation permits **もう帰らないと**（もうかえらないと）, "I have to head home."
But a negative condition with と can also lead to an ordinary consequence:
**今行かないと、間に合いません**（いまいかないと、まにあいません）,
"If we don't go now, we won't make it in time."
Do not label every unfinished ないと as an obligation without the relevant context.

## 4. Giving a direction: なさい and bare imperatives

These forms tell an addressee to act, but their social effect is not determined by a suffix alone.
Learn the forms before judging a speaker as kind, angry, male, female or entitled to issue orders.

### Verb stem + なさい

Use the **ます-stem**, not the dictionary form:

| Verb | Stem | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 書く（かく） | 書き | 書きなさい |
| 食べる（たべる） | 食べ | 食べなさい |
| する | し | しなさい |
| 来る（くる） | 来（き） | 来なさい（きなさい） |

For a godan verb, change the final u-row sound to the corresponding i-row sound;
for an ichidan verb, remove る. する and 来る have the irregular stems し and き.
The [verb-stem explanation](japanese-verb-foundations.md) gives the fuller map.

Parents, teachers and written exercises often use なさい for directions. It can also be caring,
but it is not a universally gentle request or a safe default when addressing a superior.

The shortened **stem + な** needs care:
**読みな**（よみな） tells someone to read, while **読むな**（よむな） tells them **not** to read.
The latter is the existing dictionary-form + な prohibition.
Recognize the distinction; this installment does not add a separate short-な production group.

### Bare imperative forms

| Verb class | Operation | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Godan | Change the last u-row sound to its e-row counterpart. | 書く → 書け; 帰る（かえる） → 帰れ |
| Ichidan | Replace final る with ろ, or the more literary/formal よ variant. | 食べる → 食べろ／食べよ; 見る（みる） → 見ろ／見よ |
| Irregular する | Learn the forms. | する → しろ／せよ |
| Irregular 来る | Learn the changed reading. | 来る → 来い（こい） |

帰る is godan even though it ends in える. Do not make it 帰ろ by treating it as ichidan.
The special verb **くれる** has the common imperative **くれ**, not a form obtained by mechanically
applying every ordinary ichidan example. This is recognition support, not an extra scored production facet.

Bare imperatives occur in direct orders, signs, urgent instructions and encouragement.
They are not universally hostile, and their use is not restricted to one gender.
Adding よ does not automatically make any command polite.

### Command framing versus request framing

Compare supplied utterances rather than guessing the only socially possible sentence:

| Utterance | Presentation |
| --- | --- |
| ここへ来い。 | A direction in the bare command form: "Come here." |
| ここへ来てください。 | A request-form expression: "Please come here." |
| この文を読みなさい。 | A direction with なさい: "Read this sentence." |
| この文を読んでくれない？ | In a favor-seeking conversation: "Could you read this sentence for me?" |

Read **文（ぶん）**, "sentence."
The negative question in the last example does **not** ask the person to avoid reading.
Its action is 読んで; the request uses the negative question on くれる.
See [favor questions and negation](benefactive-actions.md) for the existing full practice.

A request can carry considerable authority, and a command can be protective or encouraging.
The interpretation labels distinguish the presented **command/request framing**,
not an objective politeness score, the speaker's feelings or guaranteed compliance.

## 5. A suggestion about what to do: たらどう

Use a verb's た/だ form plus **ら**, then **どう**:

> 水を飲んだらどうですか。  
> みずをのんだらどうですか。  
> How about drinking some water?

飲む becomes **飲んだ**, so the connector is **飲んだら**, not 飲むたら.
For an ichidan verb, 見る becomes **見たら**:
**この映画を見たらどうですか**（このえいがをみたらどうですか）,
"How about watching this film?"

**～たらどう？** and a suitably intoned **～たら？** can also suggest an action.
The full form is not automatically gentle: delivery and circumstances can make it sound critical.
It is not a rule obliging the listener to follow the suggestion.
The existing [ほうがいい advice](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md#ほうがいい-recommend-doing-or-not-doing)
remains useful; these are not mutually exclusive translations of English "should."

Questions seeking instructions, such as **どうしたらいいですか**, "What should I do?",
are a related onward topic, not scored here. The exact curriculum boundary differs from this
たらどう suggestion; recognizing たら does not make every たらいい expression the same lesson.

## 6. Looking back: a preferred alternative or a welcome actual course

### ばよかった: a retrospective preference

Use the verb's **ば form + よかった**:
書く → 書けばよかった; 食べる → 食べればよかった; する → すればよかった.
For a negative preference, use **なければよかった**:
書かなければよかった, "I wish I hadn't written it."
The [ば table](japanese-conditionals.md#ば-learn-the-conjugation-including-negative-forms) explains the conversion.

> 昨日は勉強しませんでした。試験に落ちました。勉強すればよかったです。  
> きのうはべんきょうしませんでした。しけんにおちました。べんきょうすればよかったです。  
> I didn't study yesterday. I failed the exam. I wish I had studied.

Here the facts establish the missed action, and the last sentence evaluates the alternative.
For a regretted action, reverse the preference:
**夜コーヒーを飲みました。眠れませんでした。飲まなければよかったです。**
（よるコーヒーをのみました。ねむれませんでした。のまなければよかったです。）
"I drank coffee at night. I couldn't sleep. I wish I hadn't drunk it."
The positive or negative preference matters; a familiar よかった does not by itself mean the speaker is happy.

Retrospective regrets are a common use, not a license to reconstruct every past event from
an isolated conditional ending. These exercises give the relevant actual events explicitly.

### てよかった: glad about what happened, or did not happen

> 昨日は勉強しました。試験に合格しました。勉強してよかったです。  
> きのうはべんきょうしました。しけんにごうかくしました。べんきょうしてよかったです。  
> I studied yesterday. I passed the exam. I'm glad I studied.

The action is presented as actual and positively evaluated in this context.
For welcome nonperformance, use **なくてよかった** or **ないでよかった**:

> 夜コーヒーを飲みませんでした。よく眠れました。飲まなくてよかったです。  
> よるコーヒーをのみませんでした。よくねむれました。のまなくてよかったです。  
> I didn't drink coffee at night. I slept well. I'm glad I didn't drink it.

**飲まないでよかった** also fits that stated nonperformance.
Do not turn なくて versus ないで into a universal written/spoken division.
The example attributes a positive evaluation to the speaker; it does not establish a medical rule about every drinker.

The pattern also works with qualities and nouns:

| Starting predicate | Positive evaluation |
| --- | --- |
| 安い（やすい）, inexpensive | 安くてよかった — glad it was inexpensive |
| 静か（しずか）, quiet | 静かでよかった — glad it was quiet |
| 休み（やすみ）, day off | 休みでよかった — glad it was a day off |
| 高くない（たかくない）, not expensive | 高くなくてよかった — glad it wasn't expensive |
| 休日ではない（きゅうじつではない）, not a holiday | 休日ではなくてよかった — glad it wasn't a holiday |

These state/noun examples explain attachment; the new production drills target **verbs**.
よかった is the past form used for いい／よい; do not make **いかった**.
It can express gratitude or gladness as well as relief after worry. The paired interpretation tasks explicitly
set a worried speaker and use **後悔（こうかい）**, regret, versus **安堵（あんど）**, relief,
as local labels for the final evaluation. They do not redefine every てよかった as relief.

## Practice boundaries and sources

The new exercises separate no-obligation policies, necessity frames, obligation connectors and expansion,
なさい directions, bare imperatives, command/request framing, suggestions, regrets, gladness forms and
retrospective interpretation. Formation tasks require conversion or assembly; interpretation tasks read supplied
messages. These are scaffolds, not unrestricted conversational-transfer or mastery claims.
The basic prohibition and affirmative-request interpretations are N5 controls within this mostly N4 family.
Evaluation-formation prompts state their checked spelling set; this does not declare other dictionary spellings
universally wrong.

Existing requests/rules, advice and favor-question groups keep their original exercises and owners.
General concessive ても, instruction-seeking たらいい questions and harder ことはない／ほかない contrasts
are not hidden requirements of these new exercises.
