# Doing things for others and asking favors

**Who acts, who benefits, and whose side is being presented are separate questions.**
The [giving/receiving introduction](giving-receiving-and-viewpoint.md) follows the transfer of a thing.
Here **てあげる, てくれる and てもらう** present an *action* as something done for someone.

## One helpful act, two grammatical frames

> 母が私に傘を貸してくれました。  
> ははがわたしにかさをかしてくれました。  
> My mother lent me an umbrella.
>
> 私は母に傘を貸してもらいました。  
> わたしはははにかさをかしてもらいました。  
> I had my mother lend me an umbrella / received that help from her.

In both, **the mother does the lending and I receive its benefit**. くれる does not mean that
the mother received the umbrella. With もらう, the receiving side occupies the main subject/topic
position; the person marked に performs the lending. It is not safe to translate every に as "to."

Now I lend an umbrella to my younger sister:

> 私は妹に傘を貸してあげました。  
> わたしはいもうとにかさをかしてあげました。  
> I lent my younger sister an umbrella.

This presents my act as something done for her. **あげる is not an outside-the-family-only form**.
Nor must くれる's beneficiary literally be the speaker:

> 友達が妹に手紙を読んでくれました。  
> ともだちがいもうとにてがみをよんでくれました。  
> A friend read a letter for my younger sister.

Here the speaker adopts the sister's benefiting side. The friend remains the reader.
**妹は友達に手紙を読んでもらいました** presents the same help with the sister in the receiving frame.
An account from the friend's giving side can instead use **読んであげました**.
Changing the presentation need not change the actual reader.

Family and social relationships help establish viewpoint; they are not an immutable pronoun chart.
These are ordinary helpful-action accounts, not a promise to cover every ironic or marked use.
An action framed as a favor does not prove the recipient actually welcomed it.

## Keep the lexical verb's participants

| Predicate in these examples | Main grammatical frame | Person doing the lexical action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 貸してあげる | actor gives the lending benefit | the actor |
| 貸してくれる | actor gives toward the adopted receiving side | the actor |
| 貸してもらう | receiver receives the lending benefit | the person marked に |

This is not a rule that every beneficiary is marked に. The lexical verb still matters:
**妹を手伝ってあげる**（いもうとをてつだってあげる）, "help my younger sister,"
keeps 手伝う's person-object with を. Conversely, 傘を in the umbrella examples identifies the
umbrella, not the person benefiting.

**てもらう does not inherently mean coercion.** It can report requested help, accepted voluntary help
or an arranged service. It does not by itself tell you whether a request was made, refused or agreed to
under pressure. Also, do not automatically transfer the noun-gift lesson's に／から interchangeability
to every benefactive action: the lexical predicate and source interpretation matter.

## Build the action form, then inflect the last verb

Use the lexical verb's **て/で form**: 貸す（かす） → 貸して; 読む（よむ） → 読んで.
Then add the auxiliary. あげる and くれる are ichidan here; もらう is godan.

| Auxiliary | Plain affirmative past | Plain negative past | Polite affirmative past |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| あげる | あげた | あげなかった | あげました |
| くれる | くれた | くれなかった | くれました |
| もらう | もらった | もらわなかった | もらいました |

Thus **読んでくれなかった** negates receiving the reading as a favor from that person.
It is not the same structure as **読まないでくれた**, receiving their refraining from reading as a favor.
Do not infer deliberate refusal from a negative report alone.
The ordinary nonpast negatives are **あげない／くれない／もらわない**.
The [verb foundations](japanese-verb-foundations.md) supply the wider inflection tables.

The formation questions request the named auxiliary and ordinary plain past polarity, while keeping the
printed participant frame. Their kana auxiliary convention does not declare kanji spellings ungrammatical.
The lexical lending spelling **藉す** is also dictionary-recognized; the teaching model uses 貸す,
and recognition does not establish equal everyday frequency.

## Thanks and apologies: whose action are you connecting?

> 傘を貸してくれて、ありがとう。  
> かさをかしてくれて、ありがとう。  
> Thank you for lending me the umbrella.
>
> 傘を勝手に貸して、すみません。  
> かさをかってにかして、すみません。  
> I'm sorry I lent the umbrella without asking.

The first connects the other person's helpful action with **くれて + ありがとう**.
The second connects the speaker's regretted action directly with **て + すみません**.
Read 勝手に as かってに, "without permission" here.
Do not mechanically replace くれて with もらって when directly thanking the person who helped.

**ありがとうございます／ありがとうございました** and **ごめんなさい／ごめん** are useful
neighboring expressions, with register and discourse differences.
The small assembly tasks supply a particular thanks/apology ending and ask you to build its connection;
they are not an unrestricted test of every way to thank or apologize.
Gratitude and apology can coexist: someone may thank a friend for help while apologizing for the trouble.
Neither someone's job title nor the fact that a service was expected creates a universal ban on thanks.

## Casual requests: a completed て or ないで utterance

> この手紙を読んで。  
> このてがみをよんで。  
> Read this letter, please.
>
> この手紙を読まないで。  
> このてがみをよまないで。  
> Please don't read this letter.

In a request context, a bare **て/で form** asks for the action.
The ordinary negative request uses **ない-form + で**, not なくて.
These can suit familiar conversation, but tone and situation can make a short request sound direct.
Not every sentence fragment ending in て is a request: a speaker may be continuing or pausing.
The exercise explicitly establishes a request, rather than asking you to invent inaudible intonation.

The [request/rule introduction](japanese-requests-permission-and-rules.md) retains the earlier
ください requests, permission, prohibitions and obligations. Bare ないで is not automatically the
same message as a strong dictionary-form + な prohibition.

## Indirect questions: locate the negative

| Favor question to the listener | Requested action |
| --- | --- |
| 読んでくれる？ / 読んでもらえる？ | read |
| 読んでくれない？ / 読んでもらえない？ | read |
| 読まないでくれる？ / 読まないでもらえる？ | refrain from reading |
| 読まないでくれない？ / 読まないでもらえない？ | refrain from reading |

**もらえる** is the potential form of もらう. Do not replace it mechanically with
もらう when constructing these "could I receive this help?" questions.
The [ability lesson](ability-and-perception.md) explains that formation.

In **読んでくれない？**, the negative on the auxiliary helps frame a request for *reading*.
In **読まないでくれない？**, **読まないで** makes the requested lexical action negative.
A second negative does not turn that into an instruction to read.
These interpretations depend on the stated favor-question context; **読んでくれない** as a statement
can instead report that someone does not do the reading for you.

Polite questions include **読んでくれますか／読んでもらえますか** and
**読んでくれませんか／読んでもらえませんか**.
Negatively phrased questions can soften a request, but there is no universal politeness score:
relationship, intonation, wording and imposition still matter. A question does not assert compliance.
Conversely, announcing **してあげる**, "I'll do it for you," can sound patronizing when it advertises
an unwanted favor. It is not universally rude simply because the beneficiary has a particular title.

## Boundaries and onward links

The groups cover participant/viewpoint interpretation, affirmative/negative auxiliary formation,
thanks/apology connections, and casual/indirect requests.
The role labels **前者（ぜんしゃ）／後者（こうしゃ）** mean the first/second-mentioned person;
the question defines them before answering, independently of button order.
The repeated lending/reading scenes share context keys across forms and roles, not independent-conversation counts.

[Desire and outcomes](desire-actions-and-outcomes.md) retains てほしい and the optional てもらいたい
bridge: wanting an action or wanting to receive it is not proof that it happened.
More formal **ていただけませんか** is left to the honorific/humble installment, not a hidden distractor.

You may also meet **てやる**, a rougher/register-sensitive alternative in some benefactive uses, as well
as an expression of strong determination. It is optional recognition here, not an N4 scored target.
The actual Bunpro N1 page includes the benefactive sense too, while JLPT Sensei puts that use at N4;
this is a genuine curriculum disagreement, not two entirely unrelated spellings.
