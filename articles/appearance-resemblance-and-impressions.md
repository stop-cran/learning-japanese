# Looks, likeness and informed impressions

**降りそうだ**（ふりそうだ） means that rain looks likely; **降ったようだ**（ふったようだ）
can be an inference that it already rained. **お城みたいだ**（おしろみたいだ） may compare
something with a castle rather than identify it as one.
Ask what the speaker is doing: **describing an impression, making a comparison, drawing a conclusion,
describing a characteristic, or reporting outwardly shown feelings**.

These are not points on one certainty scale. Several formulations can fit the same situation without
being identical in nuance. The practice separates form-building from interpreting a supplied statement;
it does not pretend that every context allows exactly one impression word.

## 1. Build appearance-そう

| Starting expression | Operation | Plain nonpast example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| い-adjective | Remove final い + そうだ | おいしい → おいしそうだ, looks delicious |
| な-adjective | Use the base without な／だ + そうだ | 元気 → 元気そうだ（げんきそうだ）, seems energetic/well |
| Verb | Use the ます-stem + そうだ | 降る → 降りそうだ, looks likely to rain |
| Ichidan verb | Remove final る + そうだ | 落ちる → 落ちそうだ（おちそうだ）, looks likely to fall |
| いい／よい | Use よさ + そうだ | よさそうだ, seems good |
| ない, absence/not having | Use なさ + そうだ | 時間がなさそうだ（じかんがなさそうだ）, seems not to have time |

For godan stems, change the final u-row kana to its i-row counterpart: 降る → 降り.
Review [verb classes and stems](japanese-verb-foundations.md) if needed.
This is not ordinary noun attachment: **学生そうだ** is not the basic way to say someone seems to be a student.
Use a suitable 学生みたいだ／学生のようだ instead. Nor is this a mechanical operation for every word:
**かわいそう** means pitiful/poor, not simply "looks cute" from かわいい.

> この料理はおいしそうだ。  
> このりょうりは おいしそうだ。  
> This dish looks delicious.
>
> 棚の上のコップが落ちそうだった。  
> たなのうえの コップが おちそうだった。  
> The cup on the shelf looked as though it was going to fall.

The second example locates the **impression** in the past. It does not establish that the cup fell.
Plain past uses **そうだった**; polite forms include **そうです／そうでした**.
Appearance-そう can draw on sensory impressions or a situational assessment, not exclusively eyesight.
With verbs it often predicts a likely event; it need not mean that the event is about to happen immediately.

**Negative forms need their own care.** おいしくなさそうだ means "seems not tasty";
おいしそうではない means "does not seem tasty," with a difference in how negation is presented.
Verb-negative **降らなそうだ／降らなさそうだ** are both documented, while **降りそうにない**
is another ordinary way to say rain does not look likely. Do not reject every inserted さ after a verb
as impossible, or treat every adjective ending in ない as the separate absence word ない.
These negative alternatives are supporting reading, not extra scored targets here.

## 2. Match the following noun or predicate

| Expression | Before a noun | Before a predicate/phrase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance-そう | 元気そう**な**子ども, an energetic-looking child | 元気そう**に**走る, run looking energetic |
| Noun + みたい | 鳥みたい**な**声（とりみたいなこえ）, a birdlike voice | 鳥みたい**に**歌う（うたう）, sing like a bird |
| Noun + よう | 山**の**よう**な**雲（やまのようなくも）, a mountain-like cloud | 山**の**よう**に**大きい, big like a mountain |

The noun before よう needs **の**; a noun before みたい does not.
**な** connects to a noun, while **に** relates the impression/comparison to a predicate or a larger phrase.
The relevant predicate need not be immediately adjacent.

**みたい** is generally more conversational than ようだ, not a different fixed degree of certainty.
It is not **見たい**（みたい）, "want to see."
For clause-based comparisons and the existing six modifier exercises, see
[よう and its connections](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md).
Purpose-ように is a different use; not every ように means resemblance.

## 3. Read the evidence and the intended claim

Compare an inference with a resemblance:

> 隣の部屋から声が聞こえる。誰かがいるみたいだ。  
> となりのへやから こえが きこえる。だれかが いるみたいだ。  
> I can hear a voice from the next room. It seems someone is there.
>
> これは図書館だ。形はお城のようだ。  
> これは としょかんだ。かたちは おしろのようだ。  
> This is a library. Its shape is like a castle.

The first uses sound to infer a presence. The second compares the shape of an identified library;
it does not turn the library into a castle. **ようだ and みたいだ can both support inference or resemblance**.
The surrounding statement matters.

An inference can concern a past event: **地面がぬれている。雨が降ったようだ。**
（じめんが ぬれている。あめが ふったようだ。） means
"The ground is wet. It seems it rained." The visible result is evidence, not proof excluding every other cause.
Neither ようだ nor みたいだ is restricted to information received by sight or directly experienced.

Evidential/reporting **らしい** presents what available information suggests:

> 天気予報を聞いた。来週は雪が降るらしい。  
> てんきよほうを きいた。らいしゅうは ゆきが ふるらしい。  
> I heard the weather forecast. Apparently it will snow next week.

Here the source is stated. Elsewhere らしい can draw on other evidence; it does not always mean
that someone explicitly told the speaker. Nor does it assign a fixed confidence or make the report true.

| Preceding expression | ようだ | みたいだ | Evidential らしい |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Noun, affirmative nonpast | 学生**の**ようだ | 学生みたいだ | 学生らしい |
| な-adjective, affirmative nonpast | 元気**な**ようだ | 元気みたいだ | 元気らしい |
| い-adjective | 忙しいようだ | 忙しいみたいだ | 忙しいらしい |
| Plain verb clause | 来たようだ | 来たみたいだ | 来たらしい |

These examples mean "seems to be a student," "seems well," "seems busy," and "apparently came,"
under an inferential reading. Retain the relevant plain inflection: **学生だったようだ** means
"seems to have been a student"; do not put の after だった. The noun/な-adjective nonpast connectors
in the table are not a rule to insert の／な after every inflected predicate.

## 4. Typicality-らしい is a different claim

**春らしい天気**（はるらしいてんき） is weather characteristic of spring.
**子どもらしく遊ぶ**（こどもらしくあそぶ） means to play in a way characteristic of a child.
This noun-attached らしい behaves like an い-adjective: **らしく**, **らしかった**, **らしくない**.

> 今年の春は暖かい。春らしい天気だ。  
> ことしのはるは あたたかい。はるらしいてんきだ。  
> This spring is warm. It is typical spring weather.
>
> 受付の人は「お客さんは八歳です」と言った。お客さんは子どもらしい。  
> うけつけのひとは「おきゃくさんは はっさいです」と いった。おきゃくさんは こどもらしい。  
> The receptionist said the visitor is eight years old. Apparently the visitor is a child.

The first describes a characteristic; the second presents information about who the visitor is.
Do not classify every **noun + らしい** as typicality without reading its context.
**春らしくない** means uncharacteristic of spring, not automatically "apparently not spring."
Typicality can carry a speaker's expectations or values; it is not a rule that every child or every spring
must have a particular trait.

## 5. Outward feelings and desires: がる／たがる

With appropriate feeling words, **がる** presents behavior/signs associated with the feeling.
It is not a freely interchangeable replacement for every adjective:

| Starting expression | Observational verb | Current plain form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 怖い（こわい）, afraid | 怖がる（こわがる） | 怖がっている |
| 嫌（いや）だ, dislike/unwilling | 嫌がる（いやがる） | 嫌がっている |
| ほしい, want an object | ほしがる／欲しがる | ほしがっている |
| 読みたい, want to read | 読みたがる | 読みたがっている |
| 食べたい, want to eat | 食べたがる | 食べたがっている |

Remove the final い of the relevant い-adjective or たい form; use the base of the な-adjective.
For an action desire, first form **ます-stem + たい**, then replace its final い with がる.
This is not the past た-form of the original verb. がる inflects as a godan る-verb:
**がっている／がっていた**, not がている. Plain がる often describes a tendency or general behavior;
がっている often focuses on what is currently shown. Context still matters.

> 妹はその本を読みたがっている。  
> いもうとは そのほんを よみたがっている。  
> My younger sister is showing that she wants to read that book.

The sister is the one showing the desire. **妹に読んでほしい** instead expresses a wish that the sister read.
For the desire-holder/actor distinction and quotations, use
[wanting actions and outcomes](desire-actions-and-outcomes.md).
The supplied **を** in 本をほしがる／本を読みたがる belongs to the displayed verb frame;
do not mechanically carry every particle choice from plain ほしい／たい into it.

These patterns do not grant direct access to someone's mind. Quotation, narrative perspective and
self-observation also matter: do not ban first-person がる outright or replace every third-person たい
with たがる. A person can report their own behavior from an observing perspective, and a quotation can
report someone else's own たい statement.

## Practice and follow-up boundaries

The new practice contains **38 N4 tasks** in five groups: 12 appearance formations, eight modifier
connections, four inference/resemblance interpretations, four typicality/evidential interpretations,
and ten observed-feeling/desire formations. Named formation tasks request ordinary full plain forms;
they do not claim to assess unrestricted conversational production. Contextual meanings and readings
are supplied without translating the missing construction.

The six existing よう modifier tasks retain their original identities and owner. Existing desire-kind
and participant tasks remain separate. Negative appearance variants, other registers and wider
observation perspectives are explanation/recognition support, not separately assessed here.

**N3 follow-up: hearsay-そう.** Compare **降りそうだ**, "rain looks likely," with **降るそうだ**,
"I hear it will rain." The latter keeps the plain verb; with a noun or な-adjective, **だそうだ**
can appear. This is the primary curriculum's separate N3 pattern, not a required N4 distractor here.
More detailed に見える／と見える, って感じ, 風 and personality がり work is deferred rather than
silently counted as covered.
