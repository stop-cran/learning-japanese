# Only, even, and unexpected inclusion

**だけ, しか, さえ, でも, まで, and も** help you select something from a set of possibilities.
They do not all select it in the same way.

Compare these reports about a visit:

> 妹だけが来ました。  
> いもうとだけが きました。  
> Only my younger sister came.
>
> 妹しか来ませんでした。  
> いもうとしか きませんでした。  
> Nobody except my younger sister came.
>
> 妹まで来ました。  
> いもうとまで きました。  
> Even my younger sister came.

The first two can describe the same attendance: the sister came and the other relevant people did not.
The third extends the attendance to someone noteworthy in context. Perhaps the sister had repeatedly said
she could not come. It does **not** exclude the other visitors.

This is part 3 of the noun-and-particle series. Start with
[basic noun roles](japanese-nouns-and-basic-particles.md) and
[topic, contrast, and inclusion](wa-ga-mo-topic-and-inclusion.md) if the case particles or も are unfamiliar.
This lesson develops a selected set of useful contrasts, not every meaning of each spelling.

## A map: restriction is not unexpected inclusion

| Pattern | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Nだけ | Limit the relevant person, thing, amount, or other range to N. |
| Nしか + negative predicate | Exclude the alternatives to N; read the negative as part of the whole restriction pattern. |
| Nさえ / Nでさえ | Highlight an extreme or unexpected example in the relevant comparison. |
| Nでも | Can include an unlikely or minimal example; also has other uses, including suggestions. |
| Nまで | Can extend a range to a noteworthy additional member, not only mark a physical or temporal endpoint. |
| Nも | Ordinary inclusion, or an emphatic/scalar reading when the construction and context support it. |

Here **scalar** means that the context supplies an ordering: more or less expected, easier or harder,
more or less than anticipated. There is no permanent ranking of all people or things, and no universal
ladder in which は, も, でも, and さえ always have fixed amounts of surprise.

## だけ: identify the limit and its scope

[だけ](../words/だけ.md) can follow a noun directly:

> 本だけを買いました。  
> ほんだけを かいました。  
> I bought only books.

In this shopping report, the relevant purchased things were books, not notebooks or other goods.
It does not mean "I bought only one book": **category** and **quantity** are different limits.

The position matters:

| Sentence | Restricted part |
| --- | --- |
| 妹だけが本を読みました。 | Only my sister read the book: the reader is restricted. |
| 妹が本だけを読みました。 | My sister read only books: the reading material is restricted. |
| 私の妹だけが来ました。 | Only **my younger sister** came: だけ follows the whole noun phrase 私の妹. |

Read these as **いもうとだけが ほんを よみました**, **いもうとが ほんだけを よみました**,
and **わたしのいもうとだけが きました**.
The second sentence does not itself tell us whether anyone else read anything.

だけ does not require a negative predicate. It can occur with one, but that changes the proposition
whose range is restricted; see the matched negative examples below.

Nor does だけ always mean "a small amount." **好きなだけ**, "as much as you like," and
**読めるだけ**, "as much as you can read," belong to its extent uses.
The [formal-noun and particle-neighbor article](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md) covers those,
along with ばかり. ばかり can express concentration or repetition and sometimes overlaps with restriction;
it is not simply a wrong spelling of だけ.

## しか: the negative excludes the alternatives

In the complete restriction sentences taught here, **しか needs a negative predicate**:

> 本しか買いませんでした。  
> ほんしか かいませんでした。  
> I bought nothing except books.

The natural English is positive about buying books. Do not translate 買いませんでした in isolation
and conclude that no books were bought.

The negative does not have to be the literal string ない:

| Style/time | Example | Meaning in this factual report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plain nonpast | 水しか飲まない。 | I drink nothing but water. |
| Polite nonpast | 水しか飲みません。 | I drink nothing but water. |
| Plain past | 水しか飲まなかった。 | I drank nothing but water. |
| Polite past | 水しか飲みませんでした。 | I drank nothing but water. |

Read the verb forms as **のまない, のみません, のまなかった, のみませんでした**.
The plain/polite difference is not a difference between restriction and no restriction.

For existence, compare:

> 千円しかありません。  
> せんえんしか ありません。  
> I have only 1,000 yen.

In the ordinary wallet context, there **is** 1,000 yen. The statement rules out a larger available amount.
It often suggests a shortfall, but disappointment is not a grammatical requirement. A limited amount
can be welcome, expected, or simply a fact.

**本しか買いました** does not form this ordinary complete restriction pattern.
Use **本だけ買いました** or supply the appropriate negative after しか.
Conversational fragments can leave material understood; that is not permission to put an affirmative
predicate into the full しか construction.

**V dictionary form + しかない**, such as **待つしかない** (まつしかない), "there is no option but to wait,"
is a related no-alternative pattern. See the
[ほか / しか comparison](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md#ほか-exclusion-and-no-other-option)
rather than treating every occurrence as a noun followed by a case particle.

## Negative scope: three different claims

Imagine a known group invited to a meeting. These are three separate reports, not paraphrases:

| Sentence | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| 妹だけが来ませんでした。 | Only my sister did not come; the other people in the comparison came. |
| 妹しか来ませんでした。 | Only my sister came; the others did not. |
| 来たのは妹だけではありません。兄も来ました。 | It was not only my sister who came; my brother came too. |

Read the third example as **きたのは いもうとだけではありません。あにも きました**.

In the first, the restricted claim is **not coming**. In the second, the しか-negative pattern
excludes everyone except the sister from **coming**. In the third, the negative denies the
**only-my-sister limit**, not the sister's attendance.

A connected version of the third is:

> 妹だけでなく、兄も来ました。  
> いもうとだけでなく、あにも きました。  
> Not only my younger sister but also my older brother came.

The continuation makes the additional member explicit. Do not use a mechanical rule such as
"negative verb means the named person did not do it."

With other placements, especially **object + だけ + negative**, context can affect how negation
and restriction are understood. Learn the explicit subject-focused pair above before trying to
assign one English reading to every possible だけ-negative sentence.

## Keep the underlying role when combining particles

に, で, and と can keep their ordinary roles while a focus particle adds restriction or emphasis:

| Example | Role plus focus |
| --- | --- |
| 妹にだけ話しました。 | I told only my sister: recipient に plus restriction. |
| 妹だけに話しました。 | Also possible for that restricted recipient; だけ can precede the case particle. |
| 妹にしか話しませんでした。 | I told nobody except my sister: retain に before しか. |
| 図書館でしか勉強しません。 | I study nowhere except the library: activity-location で before しか. |
| 友達にさえ話しませんでした。 | I did not tell even my friend: recipient に before さえ. |
| 家でさえ勉強できません。 | I cannot study even at home: activity-location で before さえ. |

Read the less familiar words as **はなしました** (told), **としょかん** (library),
**べんきょう** (study), **ともだち** (friend), and **いえ** (home).

For the same restricted activity location, **図書館でだけ勉強します** and
**図書館だけで勉強します** are both available. Do not turn one common particle order
into a ban on the other. Their emphasis and naturalness in a wider sentence need not be identical.

For elementary subjects and objects, compare **妹だけが来た / 本だけを買った** with
**妹しか来なかった / 本しか買わなかった**. Do not build the latter by mechanically
inserting が or を between the noun and しか. Direct **妹さえ / 本さえ** patterns are
also available. This chart teaches the selected transparent combinations, not every marked or literary order.

## さえ: an extreme example, with either polarity

Suppose a teacher is normally the person who spots errors, but this time several learners and the teacher
all make the same mistake:

> 先生さえ間違えました。  
> せんせいさえ まちがえました。  
> Even the teacher made a mistake.

**先生でさえ間違えました** also fits. **さえも / でさえも** can add emphasis.
The [teacher](../words/先生.md) is presented as an unlikely person to make that mistake in this context;
the grammar does not claim that teachers never make mistakes.

Now consider a reader expected to know basic kana:

> ひらがなさえ読めません。  
> ひらがなさえ よめません。  
> They cannot read even hiragana.

This is a **negative** claim about reading hiragana. Hiragana is presented as a basic expected case,
so the failure is notable. Unlike しか, さえ does not make the negative part of an "everything except"
restriction. Compare **ひらがなしか読めません**, "they can read only hiragana":
the stated ability is different.

The contrast is contextual, not a judgment about every learner. A person may be new to the script.
Nor does "cannot even read hiragana" logically prove that the person lacks every conceivable unrelated skill.

You will also meet **すら**, and conversational [だって](../words/だって.md), in related emphatic-inclusion
uses. For the teacher example, **先生すら / 先生ですら / 先生だって** can fit too.
すら is less usual than さえ; だって is conversational.
Their other uses and combinations are not interchangeable, and sentence-initial だって can instead give
a reason or objection. These alternatives are acknowledged, not a demand to master every register now.

## でも: an unlikely example, not always an actual surprise

Suppose a short, large-print book uses language that children in the scene already know:

> 子供でも読めます。  
> こどもでも よめます。  
> Even a child can read it.

子供 is included despite a possible expectation that the task requires a more experienced reader.
This is a statement of **ability**. It does not by itself report that a particular child actually read
the book. Depending on the situation, さえ or でさえ can also emphasize such an example.

でも is common in ordinary conversation. There is no rule that it always expresses less surprise than
さえ, or that only さえ can describe something genuinely unexpected.

The same spelling has other jobs:

| Context | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| A drink is offered without excluding other choices: お茶でも飲みませんか。 | Tea or something: a suggestion, not "only tea" or necessarily "even tea." |
| Studying at school has been mentioned: 家でも勉強します。 | At home too: activity-location で plus inclusion も. |
| A new statement begins with でも、… | "But/however"; see the [word article](../words/でも.md). |

Read these as **おちゃでも のみませんか** and **いえでも べんきょうします**.
The [listing article](japanese-listing-and-examples.md#でも-one-possible-suggestion) develops the suggestion use.
Concessive **ても／でも**, as in **読んでも** (よんでも), belongs with
[concession patterns](noni-and-concessive-contrasts.md), not this noun-inclusion chart.

## まで: the range extends that far

Suppose friends have arrived for a small gathering, and a busy teacher unexpectedly also comes:

> 友達だけでなく、先生まで来ました。  
> ともだちだけでなく、せんせいまで きました。  
> Not only my friends but even my teacher came.

まで extends the set as far as this noteworthy additional person. **先生までも来ました** adds emphasis.
The surprise can be pleasant, unwelcome, or neither; the particle is not inherently a complaint.

This relates to an endpoint but is not literally a route:

> 駅まで歩きました。  
> えきまで あるきました。  
> I walked as far as the station.

The station marks an extent of walking. It is not another person joining an activity.
Likewise **五時まで働きました** (ごじまで はたらきました), "I worked until five," gives a time boundary.
Use the [basic-particle lesson](japanese-nouns-and-basic-particles.md) for until versus **までに**, "by."

Now consider a deliberately tricky question which learners get wrong:
**先生も / 先生さえ / 先生でも / 先生まで間違えます** can all include the teacher in that error pattern.
も can leave the unexpectedness to the context; さえ emphasizes an outstanding example; でも admits the
potentially unlikely case; まで presents an extension as far as that person. Several can describe the same
pattern without becoming identical in every sentence. In particular, do not assume that でも must have
this scalar reading in every bare report of a past arrival; it can also present a possible example.

## も: ordinary addition, a surprising amount, or not even one

### Ordinary addition

> 兄が来ました。妹も来ました。  
> あにが きました。いもうとも きました。  
> My older brother came. My younger sister came too.

This can be entirely unsurprising. Ordinary addition does not itself establish an expectation ranking.
With a negative predicate, **兄は来ませんでした。妹も来ませんでした** means that neither came:
the sister is added to the negative claim, not exempted from it.

### A quantity viewed as large

Suppose you expected a brief wait:

> 三時間も待ちました。  
> さんじかんも まちました。  
> I waited a whole three hours.

The amount is noteworthy relative to the expectation. This is not "three hours as well as some other
three hours." In a different scene, a large quantity may be welcome: the も does not itself prove annoyance.
Three hours is not objectively a large amount for every activity.

### With a minimum quantity and negation

> 一人も来ませんでした。  
> ひとりも きませんでした。  
> Not even one person came.

Here the negative reaches the minimum counted case, so the visitor count is **zero**.
Compare **一人しか来ませんでした**: **one** person came.
The negative endings match, but the whole constructions do not.

## One boundary worth recognizing: さえ in a condition

With the other arrangements already settled, consider entry through a station ticket gate:

> 切符さえあれば入れます。  
> きっぷさえ あれば はいれます。  
> Having the ticket is enough for me to enter.
>
> 時間さえあれば行けます。  
> じかんさえ あれば いけます。  
> If I have the time, I can go; that is the remaining condition.

This use singles out a condition as sufficient **in the relevant situation**. Do not force an "even
something surprising happened" reading onto it. Nor is "X is enough" the same logical claim as
"X is the only possible way": the sentence does not inventory every alternative route.
The [conditional lesson](japanese-conditionals.md) develops ば and related if-patterns.
Here this is a recognition boundary, not a full course in conditional さえ.

## Practice without inventing a uniquely correct particle

The accompanying practice separates:

- **Formation:** build a named pattern with the requested polarity or retained case particle.
- **Context:** allow fitting alternative framings rather than mark a natural inclusion form wrong.
- **Interpretation:** identify the stated attendance, ability, scope, or expectation from a complete sentence.

Formation restrictions appear before answering. Interpretation blanks outside quotation marks are answer labels,
not material to insert into the quoted Japanese. The questions define those labels.
Some matched scenarios share an answer class; this is not independent discrimination of all accepted particles.

Kana **だけ** and **まで** are the normal models. Recognized spellings **丈** and **迄** are accepted where
they represent these same particles; that does not make them equally common or additional grammatical choices.

**Further study:** こそ, ならでは, more detailed すら contrasts, minimum-condition formation, and advanced
restriction/negation interactions are deferred. Formal **のみ** and the broader ばかり/ほか patterns are
useful neighbors, not universal substitutes for every sentence above.
