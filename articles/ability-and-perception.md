# Ability, availability and what you can perceive

**Can read, can borrow, can see and can hear do not all describe the same relationship.**
Start with the predicate: is it an ability or available action, something visible or audible,
or a smell, taste or sound being experienced?

The [verb foundations](japanese-verb-foundations.md) introduce godan, ichidan and irregular verbs.
The [noun and particle guide](japanese-nouns-and-basic-particles.md) explains the surrounding noun phrases.
These are useful starting points, not a hard unlock requirement.

## Form an ordinary potential verb

The potential form presents doing something as possible. Possibility may come from a learned skill,
physical capacity, rules, equipment or an opportunity; the ending alone does not choose the reason.

| Verb class | Operation | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Godan | Change the final u-row kana to its e-row counterpart, then add る | 書く → 書ける（かける）, can write; 読む → 読める（よめる）, can read |
| Ichidan | Remove final る and add られる | 食べる → 食べられる（たべられる）, can eat; 起きる → 起きられる（おきられる）, can get up |
| する | Use できる | 運転する → 運転できる（うんてんできる）, can drive |
| 来る（くる） | Use 来られる（こられる） | 明日、来られる。 I can come tomorrow. |

For godan verbs, the final-kana changes are:
**う→え, く→け, ぐ→げ, す→せ, つ→て, ぬ→ね, ぶ→べ, む→め, る→れ**.
Thus 買う（かう）, "buy," gives 買える（かえる）, and 帰る（かえる）, "go home,"
gives 帰れる（かえれる）. 帰る is godan despite ending in る; it does not follow 食べる.

These potential verbs then take ordinary ichidan-type endings:

| Time and polarity | Plain | Polite ます-series |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Affirmative nonpast | 読める | 読めます |
| Negative nonpast | 読めない | 読めません |
| Affirmative past | 読めた | 読めました |
| Negative past | 読めなかった | 読めませんでした |

読めません is よめません, not よみません: 読みません says "do not read," not "cannot read."
For できる, the negative is できない; for 来られる it is 来られない（こられない）.
Learn the potential first, then inflect it, rather than attaching ない to the original dictionary form.

**Colloquial recognition:** 食べれる, 見れる and 来れる（これる） omit ら in potential uses.
You will encounter these in conversation; standard written-conjugation drills here ask for the unshortened forms.
This is a visible practice boundary, not a claim that every colloquial
potential is incomprehensible. Do not transfer that shortening to passive uses of られる.
The same-looking standard 食べられる can also be passive in a different sentence; passive interpretation
belongs to its own lesson, not a new requirement for these formation drills.

## Skill and circumstances both matter

> 妹は点字が読めます。  
> いもうとはてんじがよめます。  
> My younger sister can read braille.
>
> このカードで図書館の本が借りられます。  
> このかーどでとしょかんのほんがかりられます。  
> This card lets you borrow books from the library.

The first can describe a skill; the second an option made available by the service.
Neither says that the person is reading or borrowing at this moment.
Conversely, a past potential can occur in an account of actual success:
**昨日、やっと最後まで読めました**（きのう、やっとさいごまでよめました）,
"Yesterday I finally managed to read to the end." Do not turn potential into a rule that the action never happened.

**Dictionary verb + ことができる** is another way to express ability or possibility:
本を借りることができます（ほんをかりることができます）, "I can borrow books."
Inflect できる, not the verb before こと: 借りることができません, "cannot borrow."
The [formal-noun guide](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md) and its existing ability/experience group
already provide printer, borrowing-rule and braille practice. Those exercises stay in their original set.

### Which noun has which role?

In **妹は点字が読めます**, 妹 is the person with the ability; 点字 is what she can read.
が does not make braille a deliberate reader. With many ordinary potential verbs,
**点字が読めます** and **点字を読めます** can both be appropriate. The exercise must not reject を
merely because it contains a potential ending.

This does not make every particle freely replaceable. In **本を借りることができる**, を belongs
to the inner action 借りる, while が follows the nominalized activity. In **運転ができる**
（うんてんができる）, "can drive," が goes with the ability noun and できる.
Read the complete predicate and requested framing rather than replacing every を with が.
Topic or contrast は can also change the surface presentation without changing the real participants.

## Visibility and audibility are not just shortened potential forms

| Predicate | Basic meaning here | Compare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 見える（みえる） | Something is visible | 見られる（みられる）: can see/watch it; has the opportunity or ability |
| 聞こえる（きこえる） | Something is audible | 聞ける（きける）: can listen/hear; also can ask, with the asking sense of 聞く |

> この席から舞台が見えます。  
> このせきからぶたいがみえます。  
> The stage is visible from this seat.
>
> このサービスでは、昔の映画が見られます。  
> このさーびすでは、むかしのえいががみられます。  
> You can watch old films through this service.

The first describes a sight line. The second describes viewing access; it does not say that an image
is already in the speaker's field of vision.

> 廊下で先生の声が聞こえました。  
> ろうかでせんせいのこえがきこえました。  
> I could hear the teacher's voice in the corridor.
>
> このサイトで昨日の番組が聞けます。  
> このさいとできのうのばんぐみがきけます。  
> You can listen to yesterday's program on this site.

In **声が聞こえる**, the voice is what is audible, not a person deliberately doing something.
Likewise 舞台 is the visible thing in **舞台が見える**. は can present these things as topics;
が is the basic frame, not a rule that every sentence must visibly contain it.

This is a distinction in what the predicate presents, not an absolute attention test.
Looking carefully can help something become visible; an available recording can also be audible while it plays.
Some real situations permit more than one description. The exercises use explicit form conversion or interpretation
of an already supplied predicate rather than pretending that an underspecified "can see" has one possible translation.

## A sound, smell or taste がする

Use a **sensory noun phrase + が + する**:

> このお茶は花の香りがします。  
> このおちゃははなのかおりがします。  
> This tea has a floral aroma.
>
> スープはレモンの味がしました。  
> すーぷはれもんのあじがしました。  
> The soup had a lemon taste.
>
> 隣の部屋から小さな音がします。  
> となりのへやからちいさなおとがします。  
> There is a faint sound coming from the next room.

香り（かおり） is aroma, 匂い（におい） smell, 味（あじ） taste and 音（おと） sound.
The sensory phrase is not a cook, listener or other deliberate actor:
**味がする** describes experiencing a taste, not someone "doing a taste."
In this full frame, が follows a sensory noun phrase, not any object noun indiscriminately.

The endings belong to する: **がします**, **がしました**, **がしません**, **がしませんでした**.
With 音, a description using 聞こえる can sometimes express a related situation too.
Our formation tasks ask you to assemble the sensory する frame, not to prove that other descriptions are impossible.
Visual resemblance, **気がする** as an impression, and broader feeling expressions belong to separate discussions.

## Connect the patterns without duplicating them

- **読めるようになった**, "became able to read," builds on the potential; **読むようになった**
  can report a new reading habit. The existing change/effort practice in the
  [formal-noun guide](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md) distinguishes them.
- **読めるように**, "so that someone can read," can state an enabled goal. That goal need not be achieved.
  Existing goal tasks already use affirmative and negative potentials; the new formation work prepares for them.
- Being able to do something is not the same as wanting it. The
  [desire comparison](desire-actions-and-outcomes.md) tracks desire-holder, intended actor and wanted outcome;
  it is a meaning/role bridge, not another bank of potential-form drills.

The new practice separates formation, skill/circumstance interpretation, visibility/audibility and sensory frames.
Interpretation labels are defined before answering; their vocabulary is not a hidden prerequisite.
Familiarity with these bounded questions is not proof of unrestricted conversational mastery.
