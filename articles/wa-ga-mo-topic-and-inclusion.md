# は, が, and も: topic, contrast, and inclusion

Why can both 私は学生です and 私が学生です mean "I am a student"? Why can は and が appear in the
same sentence? And what exactly does も add?

These particles do different jobs. Learning their jobs is more useful than assigning each one a single English
translation. This article starts from ordinary noun phrases and short sentences; it does not require a separate
lesson on all Japanese particles.

## A small map before the details

A **noun phrase** is a noun together with words belonging to it: 本, "a book," or 私の本, "my book."
A particle follows the phrase it marks. The noun itself normally does not change form for its role in the sentence.

A **predicate** says what happens, what something is, or what it is like: 来ます, "comes"; 学生です,
"is a student"; おいしいです, "is delicious." It need not describe an action.

| Particle | Starting point | Do not turn it into this absolute rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| は, pronounced **wa** | Make something the topic or a point of contrast. | "Always the subject," "only old information," or "everything else is false." |
| が, pronounced **ga** | Mark the subject in ordinary action/state descriptions; also learn predicate-specific が patterns. | "Always the doer," "always new," or "always only this one." |
| も, pronounced **mo** | Add the marked item to a relevant set: this one too, or this one neither. | "A word meaning also that can be put anywhere." |

The **topic** is what the speaker sets up to talk about. The **subject** is a grammatical role within a clause:
for example, who comes or what is large. They can refer to the same thing, but they are not the same concept.

## は: set up what we are talking about

Someone asks you to introduce yourself:

> 私は学生です。  
> わたしは がくせいです。  
> I am a student.

私 is presented as the topic; [学生](../words/学生.md), "student," is what you say about it.
"As for me, I am a student" makes the structure visible, but ordinary English usually does not need "as for."

A topic can be something other than the person performing an action:

> この本は読みました。  
> このほんは よみました。  
> I read this book.

The book is the thing read, not the reader. Compare この本を読みました, where を, pronounced **o**, marks it
as the object of [読む](../words/読む.md), "read." Making it a topic does not turn the book into the person doing the reading.
The reader is understood from the situation.

Time and place can also provide the frame:

> 今日は休みです。  
> きょうは やすみです。  
> Today is a day off.

Here 今日 is the time under discussion. Do not search for a single English subject-marker translation for は.

## が: connect a subject to what happens or holds

You notice something in the garden:

> 庭に猫がいます。  
> にわに ねこがいます。  
> There is a cat in the garden.

猫 is what is there; 庭に says where. This is an ordinary existence statement, not a claim that no dog,
person, or second cat can be in the garden.

During a room tour, you notice the size of the window:

> 窓が大きいです。  
> まどが おおきいです。  
> The window is large.

The window does not have to *do* anything. が also occurs with states and properties.

### Answering who or which

Context can make the identity before が the important new contribution:

> A: だれが来ますか。  
> だれが きますか。  
> Who will come?
>
> B: 妹が来ます。  
> いもうとが きます。  
> My younger sister will come.

B supplies the requested person. In an ordinary subject question, use だれが, not a mechanically substituted
だれは. Other question roles use other particles: だれに, "to whom," and だれと, "with whom," ask different things.

The sister may already be familiar to everyone. The new information is **that she fills the requested role**,
not necessarily that this person has never been mentioned.

For contrast, suppose everyone already knows which person you are discussing, and someone asks about her occupation:

> 妹は学生です。  
> いもうとは がくせいです。  
> My younger sister is a student.

Now 妹 is the topic and the occupation is the contribution. This is a useful pair of contexts, not a rule that
every answer to a question must contain が. A speaker can reframe an answer or omit an understood phrase.

Return to the opening pair. **私は学生です** is an ordinary self-introduction: you say something about yourself.
**私が学生です** can instead identify you as the student when someone asks which person fills that role:

> A: だれが学生ですか。  
> だれが がくせいですか。  
> Who is the student?
>
> B: 私が学生です。  
> わたしが がくせいです。  
> I am the student.

English can translate both versions as "I am a student," but that does not make their conversational jobs identical.
Use the context, not just the English wording, to choose between the topic comment and the identifying answer.

The identifying reading of が can be strong in a particular question or correction. Nevertheless, **が is not
itself a general word for "only."** Context may make an identification exhaustive; do not import that conclusion
into every existence or event report.

## は and が can work together

> この店はパンがおいしいです。  
> このみせは パンがおいしいです。  
> The bread at this shop is delicious.

Think of the sentence in two pieces:

- **この店は**: this shop is the topic.
- **パンがおいしいです**: the bread is delicious.

The shop is not the bread, and the particles are not competing for one position.

> この部屋は窓が大きいです。  
> このへやは まどが おおきいです。  
> This room has large windows.

Again, the room is the topic; the comment describes its windows. Japanese 窓 does not by itself specify
singular versus plural. The translation chooses a natural reading for the example.

### A useful warning about 好き

> 私は猫が好きです。  
> わたしは ねこが すきです。  
> I like cats.

Learn the [好き](../words/好き.md) pattern as a whole. 私 is the person whose preference is being described,
and 猫 is what is liked. English uses the verb "like" with an object; Japanese 好き behaves as a な-adjective.
Do not replace が with を merely because "cats" is the object in the English translation.

Grammatical descriptions differ over how to label the が-marked item in patterns like this: some use
"subject," while others describe the target of a preference or a particular kind of object. You do not need
to settle that terminology to use the construction. What matters here is the actual pattern and meaning.
Topic or inclusion framing can change it to 猫は好きです or 猫も好きです in a suitable context.

## は can contrast; contrast does not automatically negate the other item

Both alternatives can be stated:

> お茶は飲みます。コーヒーは飲みません。  
> おちゃは のみます。コーヒーは のみません。  
> I drink tea. I do not drink coffee.

This explicitly gives different outcomes for the two drinks. The negative meaning comes from 飲みません,
not from は by itself.

Compare the shorter report:

> この本は読みました。  
> I read this book.

Depending on the conversation and emphasis, this can suggest "this book, at least" or contrast it with another
book. But the sentence alone does **not** tell us whether the speaker read that other book. They may have read
both, only this one, or be answering only about this one.

Likewise, 今日は休みです does not prove that yesterday was a workday. Topic framing makes a contrast
available; the context determines how strongly it matters and what, if anything, is implied.

## も: add the right thing to the comparison

### Another person, item, or time

> 兄は学生です。妹も学生です。  
> あには がくせいです。いもうとも がくせいです。  
> My older brother is a student. My younger sister is a student too.

も is attached to 妹. It adds another person to the people described as students; it does not say that
the sister has an additional occupation.

> ノートを買いました。鉛筆も買いました。  
> ノートを かいました。えんぴつも かいました。  
> I bought a notebook. I bought a pencil too.

Here the added item is something bought. In this basic pattern, 鉛筆も replaces 鉛筆を; do not make 鉛筆をも
your default beginner form. をも exists in more marked styles, so this is a teaching default, not a universal ban.

> 昨日は休みでした。今日も休みです。  
> きのうは やすみでした。きょうも やすみです。  
> Yesterday was a day off. Today is a day off too.

This time も adds another day. Its position tells you which part of the situation is being added.
The comparison can be understood from earlier conversation; it does not always need a preceding printed sentence.

### With a negative predicate: neither, not either

> 兄は来ません。妹も来ません。  
> あには きません。いもうとも きません。  
> My older brother is not coming. My younger sister is not coming either.

も adds the sister to the same **negative** claim. It does not cancel the negative or mean that she is coming.

> 肉は食べません。魚も食べません。  
> にくは たべません。さかなも たべません。  
> I do not eat meat. I do not eat fish either.

Read the whole predicate, not just an English gloss such as "also."

### Moving も can change what is added

> 私も学校に行きます。  
> わたしも がっこうに いきます。  
> I too am going to school.
>
> 私は学校にも行きます。  
> わたしは がっこうにも いきます。  
> I am going to school as well as another relevant destination.

In the first sentence, another person is being added. In the second, another destination is being added.
The surrounding conversation supplies the relevant comparison.

## Combining は or も with a case particle

A **case particle** indicates a role or relation such as destination, activity location, or companion.
は and も can then add topic/contrast or inclusion to that relation.

| Starting phrase | Topic/contrast | Inclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 学校に, to/at school in the appropriate predicate pattern | 学校には | 学校にも |
| 家で, at home as the location of an activity | 家では | 家でも |
| 妹と, with my younger sister | 妹とは | 妹とも |

The order in these explicit combinations is **case particle first, は or も after it**:

> 家では勉強します。  
> いえでは べんきょうします。  
> At home, I study.
>
> 家でも勉強します。  
> いえでも べんきょうします。  
> I study at home too.

The first can contrast home with another setting without telling us exactly what happens there.
The second adds home to the relevant places where studying happens.

> 妹とも話します。  
> いもうととも はなします。  
> I talk with my younger sister too.

The first と keeps the "with" relation; も adds the sister as another person talked with.

For basic subject/object phrases, the ordinary patterns use は or も in place of が or を:
猫が → 猫は / 猫も; 本を → 本は / 本も. Do not mechanically attach は or も after every particle.
Conversely, do not mechanically delete every case particle.

Case marking can be omitted in some topicalized or conversational expressions: 学校は行きます can be used
with school understood as a destination. That does not make 学校には and 学校は interchangeable in every
construction. The combination exercises explicitly ask you to **retain the supplied case particle** so that
you can practice building a transparent full form, not because every shorter expression is ungrammatical.

The で in 家でも勉強します marks an activity location. Not every written でも has that analysis:
お茶でも飲みませんか can offer tea as one possible suggestion. See
[listing and examples](japanese-listing-and-examples.md) for that different use and for repeated も inclusion.

## You do not have to repeat an understood subject or topic

Someone asks whether you read the book being discussed:

> A: この本を読みましたか。  
> このほんを よみましたか。  
> Did you read this book?
>
> B: 読みました。  
> よみました。  
> Yes, I read it.

B need not insert 私は or repeat この本. The participants and the book are recoverable from context.
The Japanese answer has no separate word meaning "I," "it," or "yes" here; the English translation supplies
what is needed naturally.

This is **omission**, not a mysterious invisible spelling of は or が. Nor does a subjectless-looking sentence
automatically mean "I": another context can supply a different person.
Dropping an understood noun phrase is also different from dropping a particle after a noun that remains spoken.
Casual speech can do the latter too, but these exercises do not ask you to reproduce every conversational omission.

## Shortcuts that help only if you keep their limits

| Shortcut | Useful part | Necessary limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "は is old information; が is new information." | A recognizable topic often introduces a new comment; が can identify the requested subject. | A person can be familiar but newly identified as the answer. Something visible or generally known can become a topic without a previous mention. |
| "は is general; が is happening now." | General descriptions often use topics; a newly noticed event often uses が. | A current question about the cat can receive 猫は今、庭にいます, "The cat is in the garden now." 今 is read いま. は is not banned from current situations. |
| "が emphasizes what comes before; は emphasizes what follows." | This can help with an identity question versus a comment about an established topic. | Ordinary が descriptions need not express exclusive focus; は can itself carry strong contrast. |
| "も means also." | It marks inclusion in a relevant comparison. | Its attachment and the predicate's polarity determine what is included and what is asserted. |

Read complete exchanges. A natural particle choice cannot always be reconstructed from an isolated English
sentence or from whether a noun appeared earlier in a paragraph.

## What the exercises do, and what comes later

The accompanying practice has six small groups:

1. Building an explicitly requested topic or が pattern, including sentences where both occur.
2. Interpreting topic-setting, identification, and inclusion in stated conversational contexts.
3. Distinguishing different stated outcomes, a shared negative outcome, and an alternative left unstated.
4. Adding people, objects, and times with も, with both positive and negative predicates.
5. Building case-plus-topic and case-plus-inclusion combinations while retaining the supplied case particle.
6. Accepting both topic and subject framings where allowed, and recognizing context-supported omission.

Formation tasks state their restrictions before answering. Meaning tasks ask for a defined Japanese label,
with its reading and English definition supplied; they do not ask you to insert that label into the quoted sentence.
The comparison label 相違（そうい）, "different stated outcomes," is not a definition of every contrastive use of は:
a contrast can be suggested even when the other outcome is left unstated.
Some questions accept both は and が and explain their different framings. This is not a claim that every context
permits either one, or that controlled form drills measure unrestricted conversational particle choice.

Later study can cover relative clauses, が/の alternation, explicit corrections, scalar も such as "as many as,"
and question-word combinations such as 何も（なにも）. Clause-linking が, often translated "but," is a different use from
the noun-following が taught here. Those are not covered just because they share a spelling.

Whole noun-like phrases can also receive particles: 日本語を勉強するのは楽しいです
(にほんごを べんきょうするのは たのしいです) means "Studying Japanese is fun."
Here の makes 日本語を勉強する, "study Japanese," noun-like, and は marks that whole phrase as the topic.
For nominalizing の, こと, and other noun-based constructions, continue with
[koto, wake, and Japanese noun-based grammar](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md).

## Sources and scope

- [Digital Daijisen: が, via Weblio](https://www.weblio.jp/content/が), specifically the case-particle entry:
  subjects of actions/existence/states and targets of preference/desire/ability are separately described.
  Historical, connective, and sentence-final uses are not treated as the same beginner pattern.
- [Tofugu: は](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese-grammar/particle-wa/): topic/comment, contrast, questions, and combinations.
- [Tofugu: が](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese-grammar/particle-ga/): subject patterns, identification, omission,
  and the stated difference between grammatical analyses of preference/stative patterns.
- [Tofugu: も](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese-grammar/particle-mo/): what is added, positive/negative inclusion,
  and case-particle combinations.
- [Bunpro: は](https://bunpro.jp/grammar_points/は): useful acknowledgement that both は and が can be natural,
  and a basic distinction between topic and contrast.

These sources use different teaching frameworks and sometimes broad shortcuts. This article does not adopt
"が always excludes every other subject," "は cannot describe the present moment," or a mandatory hidden opposite
with every は. The examples and scenarios are original teaching material; source support for a pattern is not
native-speaker certification of every invented exchange. This is a practical introduction, not an exhaustive
account of Japanese information structure or an official JLPT classification.
