# Japanese conditionals: たら, ば, なら, と, and 場合

English "if" does not select one Japanese ending. **たら**, **ば**, **なら**, and conditional **と** overlap, but they can organize the same situation differently. **場合（ばあい）** adds another useful perspective: naming the case to which a statement or procedure applies.

The practical question is not just "Which word means if?" Ask **what is being assumed, when each event happens, and what the speaker is doing with the result**: describing a tendency, making a plan, giving advice, or reporting a discovery.

## A first map, not five mutually exclusive boxes

| Form | Useful starting point |
| --- | --- |
| **たら** | If/when a condition is met; often a particular situation or an event followed by a result. |
| **ば** | The condition under which something follows or becomes possible. |
| **なら** | Given or supposing that premise; often a response to information or a proposed plan. |
| **と** | A consequence, tendency, or sequence presented as following from the first clause. |
| **場合（は）** | In the case/event of that situation; often a rule or procedure for one branch of the possibilities. |

These are learning anchors, not definitions that make every other form wrong. For example, **安ければ買います** and **安かったら買います** can both mean "I'll buy it if it is cheap." The first foregrounds the condition; the second can frame encountering that situation. That is not a different purchase decision.

## 1. Build the whole predicate, not an English suffix

The **predicate** says what happens or what something is like. It can be a verb, an adjective, or a noun with a copular ending such as だ. A **clause** is a sentence-like unit built around a predicate.

### たら: the た/だ form plus ら

| Starting form | Conditional |
| --- | --- |
| 書く（かく）, write | 書いたら |
| 読む（よむ）, read | 読んだら |
| 食べる（たべる）, eat | 食べたら |
| 来る（くる）, come / する, do | 来たら（きたら） / したら |
| 安い（やすい）, cheap | 安かったら |
| 静かだ（しずかだ）, quiet | 静かだったら |
| 学生だ（がくせいだ）, be a student | 学生だったら |
| 行かない（いかない）, not go | 行かなかったら |

Negative adjectives work the same way: **高くない → 高くなかったら** (not expensive); **必要ではない → 必要ではなかったら** (not necessary). The more conversational **必要じゃなかったら** is also possible.

**The た shape does not automatically put the condition in the past.**

- 明日、雨が降ったら、家で練習します。  
  （あした、あめがふったら、いえでれんしゅうします。）  
  If it rains tomorrow, I'll practice at home.

The rain is tomorrow's possibility, not a past fact. Time expressions and the rest of the sentence establish the time reference.

### ば: learn the conjugation, including negative forms

| Starting form | Conditional |
| --- | --- |
| 書く（かく） / 待つ（まつ） | 書けば / 待てば |
| 読む（よむ） / 行く（いく） | 読めば / 行けば |
| 食べる（たべる） / 見る（みる） | 食べれば / 見れば |
| する / 来る（くる） | すれば / 来れば（くれば） |
| 安い / いい, good | 安ければ / よければ |
| 行かない / 高くない | 行かなければ / 高くなければ |
| 学生だ / 静かだ | 学生であれば / 静かであれば |

For these plain verb forms, change the final **u** vowel to **e**, then add **ば**: 待つ → 待てば, not a general "て-form + ば" rule. For an い-adjective or a negative ending in ない, replace the final い with **ければ**.

Noun and な-adjective predicates have **であれば**, with negative **でなければ**; **ではなければ／じゃなければ** also occur. **なら／ならば** is another important pattern, discussed below, not an instruction to attach ければ to every word.

### なら and と: the presence or absence of だ matters

| Predicate | With なら | With conditional と |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 行く | 行くなら | 行くと |
| 安い | 安いなら | 安いと |
| 静かだ | 静かなら | 静かだと |
| 学生だ | 学生なら | 学生だと |
| 行かない | 行かないなら | 行かないと |
| 学生ではない | 学生ではないなら | 学生ではないと |

Use **学生なら**, not *学生だなら*. Conversely, retain だ in **学生だと**. Negative noun and adjective forms ending in ない already function as predicates; do not add another だ.

**なら** can also follow a past proposition: **もう送ったなら** (if you have already sent it). **のなら／んなら** makes the assumed situation explicit, as in **行くのなら**. With a noun or な-adjective, that expanded pattern uses **なのなら**, as in **学生なのなら**. Plain **ならば** is also possible; do not confuse it with a new conjugation of the preceding verb.

The と here is not the "and/with" と in **友達と** or the quotation marker in **行くと言った**.

## 2. たら and なら: reaching a point versus taking up a premise

Compare two instructions about the same trip:

| Situation | Sentence and meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Someone tells you about a plan to visit Japan. Preparation is still possible. | 日本に行く**なら**、出発前に少し日本語を勉強しておくといい。 — If you're going to Japan, it is a good idea to study a little Japanese before departure. |
| Someone will arrive in Japan and then contact you. | 日本に着い**たら**、連絡してください。 — When you arrive in Japan, please contact me. |

Readings: 出発前（しゅっぱつまえ, before departure）, 勉強（べんきょう, study）, 着く（つく, arrive）, 連絡（れんらく, contact）.

With **なら**, the plan is the premise for the advice. Studying may happen **before** the journey; なら does not require the first event to finish before the response becomes relevant.

With **着いたら**, arrival is the point after which the requested contact is to happen. **着くなら** would instead take the prospect of arrival as a premise; it does not by itself express "wait until you have arrived."

This is not "なら always means before, たら always means after." なら does not fix one chronological order, and a たら condition can be a state rather than a completed action:

- 暇だったら、いっしょに散歩しませんか。  
  （ひまだったら、いっしょにさんぽしませんか。）  
  If you're free, would you like to take a walk together?

Nor must a whole ongoing activity finish:

- 川のそばを歩いていたら、友達に会った。  
  （かわのそばをあるいていたら、ともだちにあった。）  
  While I was walking beside the river, I ran into a friend.

Here **歩いていたら** frames being in the middle of a walk, not completing it.

## 3. と: regular consequences, habits, and discovered results

**と** commonly presents a result as following from the condition:

- このボタンを押すと、画面が明るくなります。  
  （このボタンをおすと、がめんがあかるくなります。）  
  Pressing this button makes the screen brighter.
- 子どものころは、雨が降ると、よく家で絵を描いた。  
  （こどものころは、あめがふると、よくいえでえをかいた。）  
  As a child, I often drew pictures at home when it rained.

The first describes how this device works. The second describes a **past habit**, including a voluntary activity. Neither establishes a rule that every と sentence must describe an involuntary law of nature.

The result can also be one event the speaker encountered:

- 箱を開けると、中に手紙が入っていた。  
  （はこをあけると、なかにてがみがはいっていた。）  
  When I opened the box, there was a letter inside.

**箱を開けたら、中に手紙が入っていた** is also natural. The letter was already there: opening the box enabled the discovery, not the letter's existence. と does not always claim physical causation or require surprise.

Do not mechanically replace たら with と in an instruction such as **駅に着いたら、電話してください** ("Please call when you arrive at the station"). The ordinary conditional と pattern presents a consequence; it is not the usual way to issue that one-off request.

For a plain list of deliberate actions already completed, **てから** or ordinary て-linking may be clearer than trying to turn every English "after" into a conditional. Compare the event-order discussion in [formal nouns and time expressions](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md).

## 4. ば: a condition, not a ban on plans or requests

- 時間があれば、もう一度練習します。  
  （じかんがあれば、もういちどれんしゅうします。）  
  If I have time, I'll practice once more.
- よければ、この席に座ってください。  
  （よければ、このせきにすわってください。）  
  If you like, please sit in this seat.

**Having time** and **being agreeable** are states under which a plan or invitation applies. These examples disprove a blanket rule that ば cannot be followed by an intention or request.

The type of first predicate and the relationship between the actions matter. When the goal is "finish this action, then carry out my next instruction," **たら** is a useful starting model. Do not mechanically substitute ば merely because both can translate "if"; equally, do not label every ば + request ungrammatical.

**ば does not by itself assert that the condition is necessary, sufficient for every purpose, or guaranteed to occur.** In **安ければ買います**, low price is the stated condition for this buying decision. The sentence alone does not say that nothing expensive will ever be bought.

Negative conditions need the same care:

- 予約しなければ、この部屋は使えません。  
  （よやくしなければ、このへやはつかえません。）  
  Without a reservation, you cannot use this room.

This makes a reservation necessary under the stated rule. It does not promise that a reservation alone guarantees use: opening hours or another requirement may still matter. See also **ないかぎり** in [the formal-noun comparison](koto-wake-and-formal-nouns.md).

## 5. 場合: name the case to which something applies

[場合（ばあい）](../words/場合.md) is a noun, so its attachment follows noun-modifying patterns:

| Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Noun + の場合 | 雨の場合 — in the event of rain |
| な-adjective + な場合 | 必要な場合 — if necessary |
| い-adjective + 場合 | 高い場合 — if it is expensive |
| Plain verb + 場合 | 参加する場合 — if participating |
| Negative or past verb + 場合 | 来ない場合 / 忘れた場合 — if not coming / if something has been forgotten |

Readings: 必要（ひつよう）, 高い（たかい）, 参加する（さんかする）, 来ない（こない）, 忘れた（わすれた）.

**は** often introduces what applies in that case. It is not an inseparable piece of the noun: **場合には** is also common, and other constructions can use 場合 without は.

- パスワードを忘れた場合は、受付に相談してください。  
  （パスワードをわすれたばあいは、うけつけにそうだんしてください。）  
  If you have forgotten your password, consult reception.
- 来週参加する場合は、今日中に申し込んでください。  
  （らいしゅうさんかするばあいは、きょうじゅうにもうしこんでください。）  
  If you will participate next week, please apply by the end of today.

The second example is a **planned, controllable event**, with application before participation. 場合 is not restricted to emergencies, random events, or events already completed.

Compare:

- **忘れたら、相談してください** — if you forget it, ask for help.
- **忘れた場合は、相談してください** — in the case where it has been forgotten, ask for help.

Both can give appropriate instructions. The latter explicitly names a case and often suits procedural writing; greater formality does not make the former wrong.

Similarly, **私の場合は** ("in my case") can contrast personal circumstances rather than posit a future condition. Avoid translating every occurrence as a hypothetical "if."

## 6. Hypotheses, counterfactuals, and もし

A **counterfactual** imagines a situation contrary to what is known to have happened or to be true.

- 昨日は電車に乗り遅れた。もっと早く家を出れば、間に合ったのに。  
  （きのうはでんしゃにのりおくれた。もっとはやくいえをでれば、まにあったのに。）  
  I missed the train yesterday. If I had left home earlier, I would have made it.

**もっと早く家を出たら、間に合ったのに** can convey the same unrealized possibility. The context, past outcome and final **のに** make the regret clear; ば alone is not a dedicated "past unreal" tense.

For the relation to concessive and nominalizing uses of のに, see
[のに: unexpected outcomes, comparisons, and the other のに](noni-and-concessive-contrasts.md).

**なら** can introduce an imagined identity or situation too:

- 私が店長なら、閉店時間を変えたい。  
  （わたしがてんちょうなら、へいてんじかんをかえたい。）  
  If I were the manager, I would want to change the closing time.

**店長だったら／店長であれば** are also possible formulations. "Taking up a premise" does not mean that なら always reports somebody else's words.

**もし** makes the hypothetical framing explicit, but it does not select the following conditional for you. **もし時間があれば** and **もし時間があったら** both work. Nor does adding もし make a condition factually false or turn every clause into a counterfactual.

In **行ったなら**, a completed visit is the assumed proposition: "if you went/have gone." It need not be a regret. Compare **行ったら** in a future plan: "if/when you go." Read the whole context instead of identifying past time from a single た.

## 7. How to choose without learning false exclusions

1. **Build a valid predicate.** Distinguish 安ければ, 静かなら, 静かだと and 静かな場合.
2. **Identify the premise and timing.** Has the event happened, is somebody planning it, or does the result wait for it?
3. **Identify the main clause's job.** A habit, discovery, invitation and administrative rule may suggest different first choices.
4. **Check whether alternatives preserve the intended meaning.** A different emphasis is not automatically a wrong answer.

Formation exercises may explicitly ask for the ば form, or for a noun before a printed **場合は**. Those are controlled form tasks, not proof that the other conditionals are impossible in unrestricted speech.

For context exercises, several completions may be accepted. For example, an observed discovery can allow both **開けると** and **開けたら**; a procedure can allow both **忘れたら** and **忘れた場合は**. An explanation should say what each contributes rather than hide legitimate alternatives.

This article covers the basic conditional comparison. Set expressions such as **〜ばよかった**, **〜たらどう**, **〜といい**, **〜なければならない**, and **〜ば〜ほど** have additional functions; recognizing a familiar ending does not make their full meanings interchangeable.

For focused **なければ／ないと obligations, たらどう suggestions and ばよかった regrets**,
continue to [necessity, advice and instructions](necessity-advice-and-instructions.md).
That companion also contrasts a preferred past alternative with being glad about the actual course.

For the focused distinction between a condition and a result holding **even if/though** that condition applies,
see [ても and paired alternatives](reasons-and-contrasts.md#ても-and-paired-alternatives).
For the viewpoint of an event before とき, use [relative event order](japanese-actions-states-and-time.md#とき-and-relative-event-order).
